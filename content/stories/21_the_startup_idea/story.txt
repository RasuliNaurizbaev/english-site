The Startup Idea

Every Saturday morning, Jake and his best friend Nina used to go to the farmers' market in their small town. They would buy fresh bread, cheese and vegetables, and then they would sit in a café and talk about their dreams. Jake was a software developer who worked for a large bank. Nina had studied business and worked in the marketing department of a supermarket chain. Both of them were bored with their jobs.

One Saturday in March, they arrived at the market a little late. The man who sold the best honey had already gone home, and the bakery stall had sold all its bread. "If only we had known," Nina said. "We could have come earlier." An old farmer who was packing his boxes heard her. "That happens every week," he told them. "People don't know what we have or when we finish. And when it rains, half of them stay at home, and we throw away food."

On the way to the café, Jake was unusually quiet. Then he stopped in the middle of the street. "What if there was an app for this?" he asked. "Farmers could show what they are selling that day. People could order in advance and pick their bags up at the stall. Nothing would be wasted." Nina looked at him for a long moment. "That's actually a brilliant idea," she said.

For the next three months, they spent every evening and weekend on the project. Jake wrote the code, while Nina talked to the farmers and planned how to advertise the app. They called it "Market Basket". Not everybody was enthusiastic. Some of the older farmers had never used a smartphone, and one of them said he would rather close his stall than learn. But Nina was patient. She visited them again and again, explained everything slowly and helped them to take photos of their products.

By the summer, twelve farmers had agreed to try the app. The first version was tested by about two hundred people from the town. The results were better than they had expected. Orders were coming in every Friday night, and the farmers were selling more than before. The old farmer who had complained about the rain told Nina that he had not thrown away a single box of tomatoes for a month.

There was only one problem: money. Jake and Nina had been paying for everything themselves. The servers, the design and the advertising had cost much more than they had planned. By September, Nina had spent nearly all her savings. Jake had borrowed money from his parents, and he felt terrible about it. "We can't go on like this," he said one evening. "Either we find an investor, or we stop."

A friend of Nina's told them about a competition for new businesses in the city. Five teams would be chosen to present their ideas to a group of investors, and the winner would receive fifty thousand pounds. They sent an application, and two weeks later they were invited to take part. Nina was delighted. Jake was terrified. He had never spoken in front of a large audience before.

They prepared for the pitch for three weeks. Nina wrote the presentation, and Jake practised his part in front of the mirror. On the day of the competition, they arrived early. The other teams looked very professional, and they were wearing expensive suits. Jake suddenly felt like a schoolboy.

The pitch was a disaster. First, the projector didn't work with Jake's laptop, and they lost five minutes trying to fix it. Then Jake forgot half of what he had prepared. When one of the investors, a woman called Mrs Grant, asked him how they would make money, he couldn't give a clear answer. "Well, we haven't completely decided yet," he said. Another investor asked whether the app would work in big cities. Nina tried to answer, but she could see that they were not convinced. After ten minutes, they were thanked politely and asked to leave.

They didn't win. They weren't even mentioned at the end. On the train home, neither of them spoke for a long time. Finally, Jake said, "I wish I hadn't agreed to do this. If I had been better prepared, we might have had a chance." Nina shook her head. "It wasn't only you. We had been working so hard on the app that we forgot to think about the business. They were right to ask those questions."

For a week, they didn't talk about Market Basket at all. Jake went to work, came home and watched television. He was seriously thinking about giving up. Then, on Saturday morning, Nina phoned him. "Come to the market," she said. "I want to show you something."

When Jake arrived, the market was busier than he had ever seen it. Nina pointed at the stalls. Almost every farmer had a small sign with the Market Basket logo on it. A young mother was collecting her bag of vegetables, and she was showing her friend how to use the app. "Look at this," Nina said. "People are using it every week, and we haven't spent a penny on advertising for a month. The idea works. We just need to explain it better."

That afternoon, they sat in their usual café with a notebook. They made a list of all the questions the investors had asked, and they tried to answer every one honestly. They decided that the farmers would pay a small amount for each order, and that shoppers would use the app for free. They also found out that there were more than five hundred farmers' markets in the country. Suddenly, their small idea did not look so small.

Three weeks later, something unexpected happened. Nina received an email from Mrs Grant, the investor who had asked the difficult question at the competition. She wrote that she had been thinking about their app since that day. Her parents had a farm, and she had told them about Market Basket. They had downloaded it, and they liked it very much. She asked whether Jake and Nina would be interested in meeting her for coffee.

This time, they were ready. They brought their notebook, the numbers from the last six months and a short video in which three farmers talked about how the app had changed their week. Jake explained the business plan clearly and calmly. When Mrs Grant asked how they would make money, he smiled. "I'm glad you asked that again," he said. "We've thought about it a lot since the last time."

Mrs Grant laughed. At the end of the meeting, she told them that she would like to invest in their company. It was not fifty thousand pounds, but it was enough to pay for the servers for a year and to bring the app to three more towns.

A year later, Market Basket was used at forty markets, and Jake and Nina had left their old jobs. They had made many mistakes, and they knew they would make more. But every Saturday they still went to the market in their small town, bought fresh bread and sat in the same café. "Do you know what?" Jake said one morning. "If we had won that competition, we would never have learned so much." Nina raised her coffee cup. "To failing," she said. "The first time."
