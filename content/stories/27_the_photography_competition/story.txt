The Photography Competition

Tom Walker had been taking photos since he was twelve, when his uncle had given him an old camera for his birthday. Now he was thirty-four and worked as an accountant in a large office. His job was well paid but boring, and photography was the thing that kept him happy. Every weekend he would get up early and walk around the city with his camera, looking for interesting light, faces and streets.

He had never shown his photos to anyone except his girlfriend, Anna. She always told him they were good enough for a gallery. "You should do something with them," she used to say. "They are wasted on your laptop."

One evening in March, Anna came home with a poster. The city council was organising a photography competition called "Our City, Our Life". Anyone could enter, and the winner would receive two thousand pounds. The best twenty photos would be shown in an exhibition at the city museum.

"You have to enter," Anna said. Tom laughed and shook his head. He said that hundreds of people would take part, including professionals. But Anna didn't give up. She kept reminding him every day until he finally agreed to try.

The rules were simple. Each person could send one photo, taken in the city during the last twelve months. The deadline was the end of April. Tom spent the next few weekends going through thousands of pictures on his laptop. Some were nice, but none of them seemed special. He wished he had taken more photos of people instead of buildings.

Then, three weeks before the deadline, something happened that he could never have planned. He was waiting for a bus in the rain on a grey Monday morning. He had his camera with him because he had been planning to take photos at lunchtime. Suddenly, an old man walked past with a bright red umbrella. At the same moment, a little girl in a yellow coat ran across the street to give him a flower she had picked. The old man stopped and bent down, smiling. Behind them, the wet street reflected the lights of the shops.

Tom didn't think. He lifted his camera and pressed the button three times. Then the bus arrived, and the moment was gone.

When he looked at the photos that evening, his heart started to beat faster. The second picture was almost perfect. The red umbrella, the yellow coat and the grey street made a beautiful picture. You could see the surprise and happiness on the old man's face. Anna looked at it over his shoulder and was silent for a moment. "That's it," she said. "That's the one."

There was just one problem. On the left side of the photo, there was a big, ugly rubbish bin. And above the old man's head there was an electric wire which crossed the sky. Tom opened his editing program. Removing the bin and the wire would only take ten minutes, and the photo would look much cleaner.

He had already started to remove the bin when he stopped. He went back to the competition website and read the rules again, more carefully this time. Small changes to colour and light were allowed. But the last rule said: "Photos must show real moments. Objects must not be added or removed."

Tom sat back in his chair. Nobody would ever know, he thought. The bin was not important. It wasn't a lie, it was just a small improvement. He asked Anna what she thought.

"It's your decision," she said. "But if you won, would you be happy with that?"

Tom didn't answer. He went to bed, but he couldn't sleep. He kept thinking about his job. Every day he checked numbers for other people, and he hated it when someone changed a small detail and hoped no one would notice. In the end he got up at one in the morning, deleted the edited version and sent the original. He only made the colours a little brighter, which the rules allowed. In the form, he gave the photo a title: "A Flower in the Rain".

Five weeks later, he received an email. His photo had been chosen as one of the twenty finalists. The photos would be shown at the museum, and the winner would be announced on the opening night. Tom read the email four times before he believed it.

The opening night was in June. The museum was full of people in smart clothes, drinking wine and talking. Tom felt nervous and out of place. He walked around and looked at the other photos. Many of them were amazing: perfect sunsets, perfect bridges, perfect faces. Some of them were so perfect that they looked almost unreal.

Then the head of the jury, a well-known photographer called Mrs Hughes, stood up to speak. She thanked everyone and then said something that surprised the room. "This year we received more than eight hundred photos," she said. "Sadly, we had to remove eleven of them from the competition, including two which had been chosen as finalists. We discovered that objects had been removed or added in an editing program. Some of these photos were very beautiful. But a beautiful photo is not always a true photo."

Tom felt his face go hot. He thought about the rubbish bin. If he had removed it, his photo might have been one of the eleven.

"Our winner," Mrs Hughes went on, "is a photo that shows our city exactly as it is. It is not perfect. There is a rubbish bin in it, and there is a wire across the sky. But it shows a real moment of kindness between two strangers, and that is what this city is about. The winner is Tom Walker, for 'A Flower in the Rain'."

At first Tom didn't move. Anna had to push him gently towards the stage. He shook hands with Mrs Hughes, and everyone clapped. Later, she told him that the jury had studied his photo very carefully. "The bin actually helped," she said with a smile. "It proved to us that you hadn't touched anything."

There was one more surprise that evening. While Tom was talking to a journalist, an old man came up to him with a little girl. It was the man with the red umbrella. His granddaughter had seen the photo in the newspaper, and they had come to the museum to see it. The old man told Tom that his wife had died the year before. That morning, he explained, had been the first time he had smiled for months.

Tom used part of the prize money to buy a better camera. He printed a large copy of the photo and gave it to the old man and his granddaughter. He still works as an accountant, but now he teaches a photography class for beginners on Saturday mornings. On the first day of every course, he tells his students the same thing. "Don't try to make the world perfect," he says. "Try to see it honestly. That is much more difficult, and much more interesting."
