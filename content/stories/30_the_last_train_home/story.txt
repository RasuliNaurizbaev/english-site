The Last Train Home

Clare had been waiting for this concert for six months. Her favourite band, who hadn't played together for ten years, had announced a short tour, and the closest concert was in a city two hours away by train. She had bought the ticket on the first day, even though it was expensive. Her friend Jess had promised to come with her, but she had cancelled at the last minute because she was ill. So Clare decided to go alone.

She had checked the timetable carefully before she left. The last train home left at twenty past eleven. The concert was supposed to finish at half past ten, so she would have plenty of time. She had to be at work at nine the next morning, and she couldn't afford to miss that train.

The concert was even better than she had hoped. The band played all their old songs, and the crowd sang every word. Clare sang and danced until her voice was almost gone. But the band kept coming back for one more song, and then another. When the lights finally came on, it was ten past eleven.

Clare ran out of the hall and through the wet streets. Her phone said the station was twelve minutes away on foot. She ran the whole way, pushing past people and nearly falling twice. When she reached the platform, out of breath, she saw the red lights of the train disappearing into the dark. She had missed it by less than a minute.

For a moment she just stood there. Then she went to the ticket office, but it was closed. An old man who was cleaning the floor told her there were no more trains to her town until six in the morning. "But there's a night bus," he said kindly. "It doesn't go all the way, but it goes halfway, to a little town called Ashford. From there you can catch the first train at half past five."

Clare didn't want to stay in the big city alone, and the hotels near the station looked expensive. So she took the night bus. She was the only passenger. The driver, a large man with a grey beard, asked her where she was going. When she explained what had happened, he shook his head. "You'll have a long wait in Ashford," he said. "There's nothing there at night. The station closes at midnight."

The bus stopped in Ashford at a quarter to one. The driver had been right. The little town was completely dark and silent. The station was locked, and the only light came from a street lamp. It was cold, and it had started to rain again. Clare's phone battery was at four percent. She wished she had brought a charger. If only Jess hadn't been ill, she thought, at least she wouldn't be alone.

She walked along the main street, looking for anywhere that was still open. Everything was closed: the café, the chemist's, the pub. Then, at the end of the street, she noticed a light in a window. It was a small shop whose sign said "Books and Records". Through the window she could see an old woman sitting in an armchair, reading.

Clare hesitated. It was almost one o'clock in the morning, and she didn't want to frighten anyone. But the rain was getting heavier. She knocked gently on the glass. The woman looked up, surprised, and then came to the door.

"I'm so sorry to bother you," Clare said. She explained about the concert, the train and the bus. The woman listened, and then she smiled. "Come in, dear, before you catch a cold," she said. "I'm Margaret. I never sleep much anyway."

The shop was warm and full of old books and records. It smelled of paper and coffee. Margaret made two cups of hot chocolate and found a charger for Clare's phone. She asked Clare which band she had seen. When Clare told her, Margaret's eyes lit up. She went to a shelf and came back with an old record. It was the band's first album, signed by all four members.

"How did you get this?" Clare asked, amazed.

Margaret laughed. She explained that thirty years ago, before they were famous, the band had played in the pub across the street. After the concert, their van had broken down, and they had been stuck in Ashford for the night. "My husband and I used to run this shop together," she said. "He invited them in, just like I invited you. They sat right where you're sitting and played their guitars until morning. In the morning they signed this for us to say thank you."

Clare could hardly believe it. She told Margaret that the singer had mentioned a small town at the concert. He had said that a stranger's kindness in a little town had once saved the band. He had said that the first song they ever wrote had been written that night. "He didn't say the name of the town," Clare said. "But it must have been here."

Margaret was quiet for a moment. Her husband, she explained, had died two years earlier. Since then, the shop had been struggling. Not many people bought records any more, and she was thinking of closing it. "Sometimes I wonder whether anyone remembers this place," she said.

They talked for hours. Margaret told Clare about her husband, who had loved music more than anything. Clare told Margaret about her job in an office, which she didn't enjoy, and about how she used to play the piano before she stopped having time for it. Margaret asked her why she had given it up. Clare couldn't really answer. At some point Margaret played a few records, and they listened in silence while the rain fell outside.

At five o'clock, Margaret walked her to the station. The sky was getting lighter, and the birds had started singing. Before the train came, Clare asked if she could take a photo of the signed record and of the shop. Margaret agreed, a little confused.

On the train, Clare wrote a long post about her night. She described how she had missed the last train and how she had been welcomed by a stranger in a little shop full of records. She explained the story of the band and the signed album. Finally, she added the photos and the name of the shop. She tagged the band's official page, not really expecting anything to happen. Then she fell asleep with her head against the window.

When she woke up, her phone was full of notifications. Her post had been shared thousands of times. Fans from all over the country were asking for the address of the shop. And that afternoon, the band's singer wrote a reply. He said they had been looking for that shop for years, but they had forgotten the name of the town.

Two months later, the band played a small free concert in the pub across the street from Margaret's shop. The town had never been so busy. People came from far away, and almost every one of them visited "Books and Records". Margaret sold more records in one weekend than she had sold in the previous year.

Clare was there, of course, standing at the front with Jess. After the concert, Margaret gave her a small package. Inside was a piano book and a note. It said, "For the girl who missed the train. Don't wait too long to play again." Clare has started taking piano lessons since then. And every time she goes to a concert now, she checks the timetable twice. But secretly, she is very glad that once, she didn't.
