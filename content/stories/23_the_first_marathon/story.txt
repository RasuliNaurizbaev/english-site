The First Marathon

At school, Oliver had hated sport more than anything. He was always the last person to be chosen for a team, and he would hide in the changing room during football matches. When he grew up, he got a job in an office as a data analyst and stopped doing any exercise at all. By the age of thirty-four, he spent nine hours a day sitting at a desk and most evenings sitting on the sofa. He took the lift even when he only needed to go up one floor.

Everything changed one Monday in January. His company had organised free health checks for all employees. The nurse who examined Oliver looked at the results for a long time. "Your blood pressure is too high," she said. "And you are about fifteen kilos heavier than you should be. If you don't change your lifestyle, you might have serious problems in ten years." Oliver walked back to his desk feeling shocked. His father had had a heart attack at fifty, and he did not want the same thing to happen to him.

That evening, he told his colleague and friend Grace about it. Grace was a keen runner who had already finished three marathons. "Why don't you start running with me?" she suggested. "We could go twice a week after work." Oliver laughed. "Me? I can't even run for a bus." But Grace didn't give up. She kept asking him every day until he finally agreed, mainly to make her stop.

The first run was terrible. Grace had planned a gentle route of three kilometres in the park. After five minutes, Oliver had to stop. His face was red, his legs were burning, and he felt as if his lungs were on fire. "This is a mistake," he said, breathing heavily. "I'm just not made for running." Grace explained that everyone found it hard at the beginning. She showed him how to run slowly and walk for a minute whenever he needed to. "The secret isn't running fast," she said. "It's not stopping completely."

Oliver went back the following week, and then the week after. Slowly, something surprising happened. He started to look forward to the runs. After a month, he could run three kilometres without walking. After three months, he could run eight. He had lost six kilos, and he was sleeping better than he had for years. He had even started taking the stairs at work, which his colleagues found very funny.

In May, Grace told him that she had registered for the city marathon in October. "You should do it too," she said. Oliver thought she was joking. A marathon was forty-two kilometres. Until that spring, he had never run more than one. But that night he couldn't sleep. He kept thinking about what the nurse had said and about the boy who used to hide in the changing room. At two in the morning, he got up, opened his laptop and registered before he could change his mind.

Grace helped him to make a training plan. He would run four times a week, and every Sunday he would do a long run, which would get a little longer each week. He bought proper running shoes and started to eat more healthily. He gave up the chocolate bars he used to eat every afternoon at his desk. His mother, who had been worried about his health for years, was so pleased that she told all her friends about it.

By August, Oliver had been training for seven months, and he had just completed his first thirty-kilometre run. He felt stronger than ever. Then, one wet Sunday morning, it all went wrong. He was running down a hill in the park when he slipped on some wet leaves. He felt a sharp pain in his right knee and fell to the ground. He couldn't stand up, and a man who was walking his dog had to call a taxi for him.

The doctor told him that he had damaged a ligament in his knee. "It isn't very serious," she said, "but you must rest for at least four weeks. No running at all." Oliver asked whether he would be able to run the marathon. The doctor looked at him doubtfully. "I would not recommend it," she said. "If you had been injured earlier in the year, you would have had time to recover. Now, I'm afraid it will be very difficult."

The next few weeks were the hardest of all. Oliver sat at home with his leg on a cushion and felt sorry for himself. He stopped answering Grace's messages. He started eating chocolate again. "I wish I had never started," he told his mother on the phone. "I should have known that I'm not a runner. People like me don't run marathons." His mother was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Do you remember what you were like in January? You couldn't walk up the stairs without stopping. Look at what you have already done."

Her words stayed with him. The next day, he called Grace and apologised for ignoring her. She came round with a bag of fruit and a plan. She had spoken to a physiotherapist who was a friend of hers. He had told her that Oliver could not run, but he could swim and ride an exercise bike, and this would help him to keep fit without hurting his knee. Oliver started going to the swimming pool every morning before work. It was boring, but it was better than doing nothing.

After five weeks, the physiotherapist examined his knee and said that he could start running again, very slowly. There were only four weeks left before the marathon. Oliver knew that he was not as fit as he had been in August. He asked the physiotherapist directly whether he should run or not. The physiotherapist said that it was his decision, but that if he ran, he must go slowly and stop immediately if his knee hurt. "Your goal isn't a good time," he said. "Your goal is to finish."

On the morning of the marathon, it was cold and grey. Thousands of runners were waiting at the start line, and music was playing loudly. Oliver felt sick with nerves. Grace gave him a hug. "Remember," she said, "it's not about running fast. It's about not stopping completely." Then the race began.

The first twenty kilometres went better than he had expected. He ran slowly and steadily, and his knee felt fine. Crowds of people stood along the streets, shouting and clapping for runners they didn't even know. At kilometre thirty, however, his legs became heavy, and his knee started to ache. At kilometre thirty-five, he stopped and walked. He was sure that he would have to give up.

Then he heard someone call his name. His mother was standing at the side of the road, holding a sign that said "GO OLIVER! YOU ARE A RUNNER!" Beside her were half the people from his office, including his manager, who had never said a kind word to him in four years. Oliver felt tears in his eyes. He started running again, slowly, one step at a time.

He crossed the finish line five hours and forty minutes after he had started. Many people had finished much earlier, but he did not care at all. A volunteer put a medal around his neck, and Grace, who had finished an hour before him, ran over and hugged him. "You did it," she said. Oliver couldn't speak. He just held the medal and smiled.

Now, the medal hangs on the wall above Oliver's desk at work. Sometimes new colleagues ask him about it, and he tells them the story. He always ends it the same way. "Don't wait until you are ready," he says. "Just start, and don't stop completely."
