The Family Restaurant

The restaurant was called Rossi's, and it had been standing on the corner of Station Road for almost fifty years. It had been opened by Giorgio Rossi in 1976, when he had just arrived in England with one suitcase and his mother's recipes. Now Giorgio was seventy-eight, and the restaurant was run by his son, Marco. Marco's daughter, Elena, who was twenty-two, worked there at weekends while she was studying business at university.

When Elena was a child, she used to sit in the kitchen after school and watch her grandfather make pasta by hand. The whole street would smell of garlic and fresh bread. In those days the restaurant was always full. People had to book a table a week in advance, and on Friday nights there was a queue outside the door.

But things had changed. A new shopping centre had been built on the other side of town, with ten modern restaurants, bright lights and free parking. Fewer people came to Station Road in the evenings. Some nights, only four or five tables were taken. The walls hadn't been painted for years, the menu was printed on old yellow paper, and the restaurant didn't even have a website.

One Sunday evening, after the last customers had left, Elena sat down at a table with her father and grandfather. She had been thinking about this conversation for weeks. "We need to modernise," she said. "We need a website, online booking and a new menu with some vegetarian dishes. And we should repaint the walls. Everything looks so old."

Her grandfather put down his glass of wine. "Old?" he said. "People come here because it is old. They come for the real taste of Italy, not for fashion."

"Grandad, nobody comes here any more," Elena replied. "Last Tuesday we had six customers. Six! If we don't change something, we'll have to close within a year."

Marco rubbed his tired eyes. He was stuck in the middle, as always. He agreed with his daughter about the website, but he didn't want to hurt his father. "Maybe we could make a few small changes," he suggested. "Slowly. Step by step."

"Small changes won't be enough," Elena said. Giorgio stood up and walked into the kitchen without saying another word. The conversation was over, at least for that night.

For the next two weeks, nobody mentioned the subject again. Then, one Thursday morning, Elena was checking her phone on the bus when she saw it. Someone had written a review of Rossi's on a popular restaurant website. It had only one star.

The title said, "A sad place that has forgotten how to cook." The writer complained that the soup had been cold, the waiter had been rude and the dining room had smelled of old carpet. "This was probably a good restaurant twenty years ago," the review ended. "Now I wouldn't recommend it to anyone. I wish I had gone to the shopping centre instead."

Elena felt sick. By lunchtime the review had been read by hundreds of people, and some of them had added comments. One person wrote that they had been planning a birthday dinner at Rossi's but had changed their mind.

That evening she showed the review to her father. Marco read it twice in silence. Then he admitted something she hadn't known. "The waiter was me," he said quietly. "It was that Saturday when the chef was ill. I had been working since six in the morning, and I was cooking and serving at the same time. I suppose I was rude. And the soup probably was cold."

They decided they had to show the review to Giorgio. Elena expected him to be angry. She thought he would say that the writer knew nothing about Italian food. Instead, the old man read the review slowly, put on his glasses and read it again. Then he sat for a long time without speaking.

"The carpet," he finally said. "Is it true? Does it smell?"

Marco and Elena looked at each other. "A little," Marco said. "Yes."

Giorgio nodded. "When I opened this place, my mother told me something. She said a restaurant is like a home. If you invite people into your home, it must be clean and warm, and you must be happy to see them. I think I forgot that. I was so proud of the past that I stopped looking at the present." He looked at his granddaughter. "If you had shown me this a year ago, I would have been too proud to listen. Tell me your plan."

Elena couldn't believe what she was hearing. She ran to get her laptop. That night the three of them stayed up until two in the morning, making a list. They agreed to keep Giorgio's famous recipes, but to add three vegetarian dishes. They agreed to take out the old carpet and paint the walls a warm cream colour. Elena would build a website and look after social media. Marco would hire an extra waiter for Saturdays, so that he wouldn't have to do two jobs at once.

The restaurant was closed for three weeks while the work was being done. Friends and neighbours came to help. Elena's university friends painted the walls, and an old customer who was a carpenter repaired the chairs for free. Giorgio insisted on doing something too. He spent every afternoon writing the new menu by hand, in beautiful old-fashioned letters, and Elena scanned it for the website.

There was one more thing that Elena wanted to do. She found the email address of the person who had written the bad review, and she wrote them a polite message. She thanked them for their honest opinion and explained that the restaurant was changing. She invited them to come back for a free dinner on the opening night.

She didn't really expect an answer. But two days later a reply arrived. The writer, whose name was Mrs Clarke, said she was surprised. "Most restaurants just argue with me," she wrote. "I would be happy to come."

The opening night was a Friday in October. Every table was full. Elena had posted photos of the new dining room online, and lots of people had booked through the new website. Giorgio stood at the door in his best suit, shaking hands with every guest, as he used to do many years ago.

Mrs Clarke came at eight o'clock with her husband. She ordered the soup, of course. Marco brought it to the table himself and apologised for her last visit. Later, when she was leaving, she told Giorgio that the lasagne was the best she had ever eaten.

A week later, a new review appeared online. It had five stars. "I was wrong about Rossi's," Mrs Clarke wrote. "Or maybe I was right, and they listened. This family has taught me that it is never too late to change."

Now, a year later, Rossi's is busy again. On Friday nights there is sometimes a queue outside the door. Giorgio still comes in every afternoon to make the pasta by hand. He has even started to enjoy reading the online reviews, and he asks Elena to read the new ones to him every morning. "Without that terrible review," he often says, "we would have closed. Sometimes bad news is the best thing that can happen to you."
