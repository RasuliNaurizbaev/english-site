The Neighbour's Secret

When Anna and her ten-year-old son, Ben, moved into the old building on Park Street, everything seemed perfect. Their flat was small but bright, and the rent was cheap. The neighbours were quiet, and there was a little garden behind the house where Ben could play football.

Above them lived an old man called Mr Harris. He was about eighty, tall and thin, with white hair and a grey coat that he wore even in summer. Anna saw him only on the stairs. He always said "Good morning" politely, but he never stopped to talk. Ben thought he looked a bit scary.

About two weeks after they had moved in, Anna woke up in the middle of the night. She looked at the clock. It was ten past two. Somewhere above her, there was a strange noise. Knock, knock, knock. Then silence. Then a long, low sound, like something heavy was being pulled across the floor.

The next night, it happened again. And the night after that. Every night, between one and three o'clock, the noises came from Mr Harris's flat. Sometimes Anna also heard quiet music from an old radio.

At breakfast, Ben looked tired. "Mum, did you hear it?" he whispered. "What is he doing up there at night?"

"I don't know," Anna said. "Maybe he can't sleep."

But Ben had his own ideas. He had been reading too many detective stories. "Maybe he's hiding something," he said. "Or maybe he's building a machine." Anna laughed, but secretly she was a little worried too.

She decided to ask the other neighbours. Mrs Patel, who lived on the ground floor, had been in the building for twenty years. "Oh, Mr Harris is a lovely man," she said. "But he has been very lonely since his wife died last year. He used to go out every day. Now he hardly leaves his flat." She didn't know anything about the noises. "I sleep very well," she said with a smile. "I don't hear anything."

A week later, something else happened. Anna was coming home from work when she saw Mr Harris on the stairs. He was carrying a big bag, and it looked very heavy. Something long and wooden was sticking out of the top. When he saw Anna, he quickly turned away and went up to his flat. He closed the door without saying a word.

"You see?" Ben said that evening. "He's definitely hiding something."

On Saturday, Anna had to go to the post office. When she came back, she found a small paper package in front of their door. It had fallen out of somebody's bag. Inside there were some small pieces of wood, a tube of glue and a piece of paper with drawings. The drawings showed a little house with windows, stairs and tiny furniture. At the bottom, someone had written "For the children – St Mary's" in old-fashioned handwriting.

Anna looked at the package for a long time. Then she said, "Ben, I think we should give this back to Mr Harris."

They went upstairs together and knocked on his door. For a moment, nothing happened. Then they heard slow footsteps, and the door opened a little. Mr Harris looked surprised to see them.

"I think this is yours," Anna said, and she held out the package. "We found it downstairs."

The old man's face changed. "Oh, thank you," he said. "I've been looking for it all morning." He hesitated for a second. Then he opened the door wider. "Would you like to come in? I can explain the noise. I'm sure you've heard it."

Anna and Ben stepped inside, and Ben's mouth fell open. The living room was full of wooden toys. There were trains, cars, boats and animals on every shelf. In the middle of the room, on a big table, stood a beautiful doll's house. It was almost as tall as Ben, with three floors, a red roof and little lights in the windows.

"I used to be a carpenter," Mr Harris said quietly. "My wife, Mary, was a nurse at St Mary's children's hospital for thirty years. Every Christmas, I made toys for the children there. She always said that a toy could make a sick child forget the pain for an hour." He stopped and looked at a photo on the wall. It showed a smiling woman in a nurse's uniform.

"After she died, I stopped. I didn't want to do anything," he continued. "But a few months ago, I decided that she would be sad if the children didn't get their toys this year. So I started again. I can't sleep well at night, so I work then. I'm sorry about the noise. I've been moving the doll's house across the room to paint it."

Anna felt tears in her eyes. "Please don't be sorry," she said. "We thought you were a mystery. Now we know you're a hero."

Ben was looking at a small wooden train. "Mr Harris," he asked shyly, "could you teach me how to make one?"

The old man smiled for the first time. It was a warm, wide smile. "If you come on Saturday afternoons, I'll teach you," he said. "But only in the daytime. Your mother needs her sleep."

From that week, Ben went upstairs every Saturday. He learned how to cut wood, use glue and paint small details. Anna sometimes joined them and brought cake. Even Mrs Patel came up once to help with the doll's house curtains.

In December, they all went to St Mary's hospital together. The children's faces lit up when they saw the boxes of toys. A little girl with a bandage on her arm held the doll's house and refused to let it go.

On the way home, Mr Harris walked slowly between Anna and Ben. "Mary would be happy," he said. "This year, I didn't do it alone."

The noises at night have stopped now. Mr Harris sleeps better, he says. But on Saturday afternoons, the building is full of the sound of knocking, laughing and an old radio playing music.
