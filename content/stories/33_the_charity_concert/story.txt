The Charity Concert

The Sunflower Children's Centre had been open for almost thirty years. It was a small building with a yellow door, at the end of a quiet street in Bristol, where children from poor families could come after school. They did their homework there, played games, ate a hot meal and talked to adults who listened to them. For many of them, it was the safest place they knew.

Anna Clarke worked in the accounts department of a large travel company nearby. She had a special reason to care about the centre. When she was a child, she had gone there every afternoon, because her mother worked late and there was nobody at home. The woman who ran it, Mrs Patel, had helped her with her maths and had taught her to believe in herself.

One Monday in April, Anna read in the local newspaper that the centre was going to be closed. The council had cut its budget, and the building needed a new roof, which would cost twelve thousand pounds. If the money wasn't found by the end of June, the doors would be locked for good.

That lunchtime, Anna told her colleagues about it. "I wish I could do something," she said. "But I don't have twelve thousand pounds."

"None of us do," said Tom from IT. "But together we could raise it." Tom played the guitar in a band at weekends. He suggested organising a charity concert. Everyone thought it was a brilliant idea. Grace from marketing offered to design the posters. Raj, who was good with numbers, agreed to deal with the tickets. Anna was chosen to be in charge of everything.

At first, everything went smoothly. Anna phoned the manager of the town hall, who agreed to let them use the main hall for free on the last Saturday in June. Tom's band agreed to play, and so did a choir from a local church and a young singer called Mia, who had become famous after appearing on a television talent show. Grace's posters were put up all over the town. Within two weeks, three hundred tickets had been sold.

Then the problems started.

First, the town hall called. A pipe had burst in the main hall, and the floor had been badly damaged. It would be repaired, but not before July. Anna spent three days phoning every possible place in the town. In the end, the head teacher of a secondary school agreed to let them use the school sports hall. It wasn't beautiful, but it was big enough.

A week later, Mia's manager sent an email. He said that Mia had been offered a performance at a festival in London on the same night, and that she was very sorry, but she couldn't come. Anna read the email twice and then put her head on her desk. Many people had bought tickets only because they wanted to see Mia. "If I had signed a contract with her," she said to Tom, "she couldn't have done this."

"It isn't your fault," Tom told her. "Let's think about who else could play."

The worst moment came ten days before the concert. Raj came into the office looking pale. He explained that the website they had used for the tickets had made a mistake. Instead of three hundred tickets, it had sold four hundred and fifty, but the sports hall could only hold four hundred people. Anna felt like crying. She asked Raj how something like that could have happened. He said he had no idea, but that the company had promised to give refunds to anyone who wanted one.

That evening, Anna went to the children's centre. She had been working on the concert for two months, and she was exhausted. She told Mrs Patel, who was now in her seventies, that she was thinking of giving up. "Maybe it was a stupid idea," she said. "Everything is going wrong."

Mrs Patel took her hand. "Do you remember when you were ten and you failed your maths test?" she asked. "You told me you would never understand fractions. Two weeks later, you got the highest mark in your class. You have never been someone who gives up, Anna."

Then a small boy called Jayden, who had been listening, pulled Anna's sleeve. "We could sing," he said. "We've been practising a song for the summer party."

Anna looked at him, and suddenly she had an idea. Instead of a famous singer, the children from the centre would perform. The audience would see exactly who they were helping. And for the extra people, they could open the doors at the back of the hall and put chairs in the playground outside, where speakers would be set up.

The day of the concert arrived. Of course, more things went wrong. Tom's car broke down on the way, and he had to come by bus with his guitar. The church choir got lost and arrived forty minutes late. At four o'clock, it started to rain, and the chairs in the playground had to be covered with plastic sheets. Anna ran from one problem to another, and she didn't stop to eat or drink all afternoon.

But at seven o'clock, the sports hall was full. People were standing along the walls and sitting on the floor. Outside, under umbrellas, another hundred people were waiting to listen.

The choir sang first, and then Tom's band played for an hour. People were dancing between the chairs. Finally, Anna went onto the stage and explained why they were all there. She told them about the yellow door and about the woman who had helped a lonely little girl with her maths. Her voice was shaking, but she managed to finish.

Then twenty children walked onto the stage, holding hands. Jayden stood at the front. They sang a simple song about friendship, a little out of tune, and some of them forgot the words. By the end, half of the audience was in tears. When the song finished, everyone stood up and clapped for almost five minutes.

After the concert, Raj counted the money. With the tickets, the donations and the cakes that had been sold at the door, they had raised nine thousand four hundred pounds. It was a fantastic result, but it wasn't enough. Anna tried to smile, but she was disappointed. Almost three thousand pounds were still missing.

On Monday morning, a man in a grey suit came into the office and asked for Anna. He introduced himself as the owner of a local building company. He had been sitting in the playground on Saturday, under an umbrella, and he had listened to the children singing. "I went to that centre too, when I was a boy," he said. "My company will repair the roof. You won't have to pay for the work, only for the materials."

The roof was finished at the end of July. The Sunflower Children's Centre didn't close, and the money which remained was used to buy new books and computers.

Anna, Tom, Grace and Raj had learned a lot from the experience. Organising a concert was much harder than they had imagined, and almost nothing had gone according to plan. But when Anna walked past the yellow door now and heard children laughing inside, she knew that every difficult moment had been worth it. In fact, they were already talking about next year's concert.
