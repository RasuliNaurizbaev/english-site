The Climate Project

Ms Okafor had been teaching science at Riverside High School for ten years, and she was known for her unusual projects. One Monday in September, she wrote one word on the board: WASTE. Then she turned to her class of sixteen-year-olds.

"For the next six weeks, you will work in groups," she said. "Your task is to find out how much rubbish our school produces and to suggest a way to reduce it. The best project will be presented to the head teacher."

Most of the class groaned. Maya Collins, who sat at the back, groaned the loudest. She had been hoping for something more interesting, like space or robots. She was put in a group with three other students: Leo, who never stopped talking; Priya, who was very organised; and Sam, who rarely said anything at all.

At their first meeting, nobody knew where to start. Leo suggested making a poster. Priya said that a poster wouldn't change anything. Finally, Sam spoke. "Why don't we actually count the rubbish?" he asked. "Then we'll have real numbers."

So for one week, the group stayed after school and went through the school bins. They wore gloves and old clothes and sorted everything into piles. It was disgusting work, but the results were shocking. In just five days, the school had thrown away more than two thousand plastic cups, nine hundred plastic bottles and over sixty kilograms of food from the canteen. Most of the plastic could have been recycled, but it had all been put in the same bins.

"I didn't realise it was so bad," Maya admitted. She took photos of everything. Priya put the numbers into charts, and Leo, who loved talking, offered to present the results.

Their project won easily. When the head teacher, Mr Barker, saw the photos, he was surprised. He agreed to their suggestions immediately. Plastic cups were removed from the canteen, recycling bins were put in every corridor, and students were encouraged to bring their own water bottles. Within two months, the school's rubbish had been reduced by almost half.

That should have been the end of the story. But one Saturday, Maya was walking through the town centre when she noticed the bins outside the shopping centre. They were full of the same things: plastic cups, bottles and takeaway boxes. She counted the cafés on the main street. There were fourteen, and every one of them used plastic cups.

On Monday, she told the others what she had seen. "We changed our school," she said. "What if we tried to change the town?"

Leo laughed. "We're sixteen. Nobody will listen to us."

"If nobody tries, nothing will ever change," Priya replied. Sam nodded. In the end, Leo agreed too, mostly because he didn't want to be left out.

Ms Okafor was delighted when they asked for her help. She explained that the town council made decisions about rubbish and recycling. Any citizen could present an idea at a council meeting, but they would need strong evidence and public support. "You'll need to show that people in the town want this," she told them.

For the next three months, the four friends spent almost all their free time on the campaign. They visited every café on the main street. They asked the owners whether they would be willing to stop using plastic cups. Some were interested, but many of them were worried about the cost. One owner, a grumpy man called Mr Hale, told them to go away and stop wasting his time.

The group didn't give up. Priya found out that a town in the north had introduced a similar rule two years earlier. She wrote to their council and asked how it had worked. The reply was very helpful: the cafés there had actually saved money, because customers who brought their own cups were given a small discount and came back more often.

Meanwhile, Maya made a short video about the waste in their town, which was shared online thousands of times. Leo, who was finally using his talking skills for something useful, spoke on the local radio. Sam designed a petition, and they collected signatures outside the supermarket every Saturday morning. By February, more than three thousand people had signed it.

The council meeting was held on a cold evening in March. The room was full. Maya's hands were shaking as she stood up to speak. She had practised her speech many times, but now she could hardly remember the first sentence. She looked at her parents in the second row, took a deep breath and began.

She explained what they had found in the school bins and how the school had changed. She showed the photos of the bins in the town centre. Priya presented the numbers from the northern town, and Sam handed every council member a copy of the petition. Then Leo surprised everyone. He invited a guest to speak: Mr Hale, the grumpy café owner.

Mr Hale stood up slowly. He admitted that he had been rude to the students at first. "But they kept coming back," he said. "In the end, I tried their idea for a month. My customers liked it, and I spent less money on cups. I wish I had listened to them earlier."

The councillors asked a lot of questions. One of them wanted to know who would pay for the new recycling bins. Another asked whether small businesses would suffer. The students answered as well as they could. When they didn't know an answer, they said so honestly and promised to find out.

Two weeks later, Ms Okafor came into the classroom holding a letter. The council had voted to ban single-use plastic cups in all cafés in the town centre from the following January. They had also agreed to put recycling bins on every main street. The rule would be called the Riverside Rule, after the school.

The class cheered. Leo jumped onto his chair, and even Sam smiled widely. Maya couldn't stop thinking about the first day of the project, when she had been so bored. If Ms Okafor hadn't written that word on the board, none of this would have happened.

The rule came into force the next January, and a year later the town's rubbish had been reduced by thirty per cent. Other towns started asking the students for advice. Maya, who had once wanted to study robots, decided to study environmental science at university instead.

On her last day at Riverside High, she went to say goodbye to Ms Okafor. She asked her whether she had expected the project to go so far. Ms Okafor laughed. "Honestly, no," she said. "I just wanted you to count some rubbish. You did the rest yourselves."
