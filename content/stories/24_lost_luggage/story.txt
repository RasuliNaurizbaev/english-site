Lost Luggage

Daniel Ross had worked as a sales manager for a small company that made medical equipment for nearly ten years. He was good at his job, but he had never had a chance like this one. A large hospital group in Lisbon was looking for a new supplier, and Daniel's company had been invited to present its products. If the hospital chose them, it would be the biggest contract in the company's history. His boss had told him that he trusted him completely. That made Daniel even more nervous.

He had prepared for weeks. He had practised the presentation so many times that he could do it in his sleep. In his suitcase, he had packed his best suit, a new white shirt and, most importantly, a small sample of the company's newest product, a portable heart monitor. He had decided to put the sample in his suitcase because he was worried that it would be stopped at security if he took it in his hand luggage.

His flight left on Wednesday afternoon, and the presentation was at nine o'clock on Thursday morning. The flight was delayed for an hour, but Daniel didn't mind. He used the time to read his notes again. When he landed in Lisbon, it was already dark. He waited at the baggage carousel with the other passengers. One by one, they collected their bags and left. After forty minutes, the carousel stopped. Daniel was standing there alone. His suitcase had not arrived.

He went to the lost luggage office, where a tired young woman was sitting behind a desk. She typed his name into her computer and frowned. "I'm sorry, sir," she said. "It seems that your suitcase was put on the wrong plane in London. At the moment, we think it is in Lyon." Daniel stared at her. "In France? When will it be here?" She explained that it would probably be sent to Lisbon the next afternoon. "But my presentation is tomorrow morning," Daniel said. "Everything I need is in that suitcase." The woman gave him a form to fill in and a small bag with a toothbrush and a T-shirt. "I really am sorry," she said. "I wish I could do more."

It was nearly midnight when Daniel arrived at his hotel. He was wearing jeans, an old jumper and trainers, which he had put on for the flight because they were comfortable. He sat on the bed and looked at himself in the mirror. He couldn't present to the directors of a hospital group dressed like this. And without the heart monitor, he could only show them pictures. If only he had put the sample in his hand luggage, he thought. He would have had to explain it at security, but at least he would have it now.

He called his boss, Mrs Hall, in London. He expected her to be angry, but she was calm. "Don't panic," she said. "You know the product better than anyone. The suit is not important. Do what you can, and call me after the meeting." Her words helped a little, but Daniel still couldn't sleep.

At seven in the morning, he went down to the reception desk. A young man called Tiago was working there. Daniel explained what had happened and asked whether there was a clothes shop nearby. Tiago looked at his watch. "Most shops open at ten," he said. "That's too late for you." He thought for a moment. Then he picked up the phone and spoke quickly in Portuguese. When he put the phone down, he was smiling. "My uncle has a small tailor's shop two streets from here," he said. "He usually opens at nine, but he will open early for you. He says he has some suits that people ordered but never collected."

Twenty minutes later, Daniel was standing in a tiny shop full of fabric, while an old man with glasses was measuring his arms. Tiago's uncle didn't speak much English, but he didn't need to. He brought out a dark grey suit, looked at Daniel, shook his head and brought another one. The second suit fitted almost perfectly. He took in the trousers a little, ironed a white shirt and even lent Daniel a tie. When Daniel tried to pay, the price he was asked for was much lower than he had expected. "For good luck," the old man said in careful English.

Daniel still had one problem: the heart monitor. In the taxi to the hospital, he called a colleague in London and asked her to send him the newest video of the product. He had been planning to use the real sample, so he hadn't put the video in his presentation. His colleague promised to email it to him immediately. The video arrived as the taxi stopped outside the hospital.

At the reception, a woman told him that the meeting room was on the fifth floor. As he was waiting for the lift, a tall man in a white coat started a conversation with him. He asked Daniel where he was from and why he had come. Daniel was so nervous that he told him everything: the lost suitcase, the tailor, the borrowed tie. The man laughed. "That is quite a story," he said. "Good luck with your presentation." Then he got out on the third floor.

The meeting room was large and bright, and six people were sitting around the table. The director, a serious woman called Dr Ferreira, invited Daniel to begin. He started by apologising that he could not show them the real product. He explained that it had been sent to France by mistake. A few of the directors smiled. Then he showed them the video and talked about the heart monitor in detail. He explained how it worked, how much it cost and how it could be used by patients at home. He answered all their questions clearly. For the first time since he had landed, he felt confident.

Near the end of the meeting, the door opened, and the tall man from the lift came in. He sat down and listened. Daniel realised with a shock that he was one of the hospital's senior heart doctors. When Daniel had finished, the doctor spoke. "This morning, Mr Ross told me that he had lost his suitcase and that he had found a suit in a few hours with the help of strangers," he said. "I think that a person who can solve problems like that will also solve our problems if something goes wrong with his equipment. That matters to me more than a product sample."

Dr Ferreira said they would make their decision within a week. Daniel thanked them and left the building. He felt that the meeting had gone well, but he didn't want to hope too much.

That afternoon, his suitcase was finally delivered to the hotel. He opened it and found the heart monitor, safe and undamaged. He took it back to the hospital the next morning so that the doctors could test it before he flew home. On the way to the airport, he stopped at the tailor's shop to return the tie. Tiago's uncle refused to take it. "Keep it," he said. "Next time, you will tell me if it was lucky."

Eight days later, Mrs Hall called him into her office. The hospital group had signed the contract. Daniel's company would supply heart monitors to twelve of its hospitals for the next five years. "Well done," she said. "Next time, though, maybe pack a spare shirt in your hand luggage."

Daniel laughed. He still wears the grey tie whenever he has an important meeting. And every Christmas, he sends a card to a small tailor's shop in Lisbon, thanking the strangers who had helped him when everything had gone wrong.
