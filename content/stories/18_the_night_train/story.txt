The Night Train

Anna Clark was thirty-two and worked as a manager for a big company in London. She was always busy. She had meetings from morning to evening, she answered emails on the bus, and she never took a real holiday. Her friends often told her that she worked too much, but Anna didn't listen.

In March, her company sent her to a conference in Vienna. The conference was a success, and on Friday evening she had a flight back to London. On Saturday morning she had an important meeting with a new client, so she couldn't be late.

But everything went wrong that afternoon. Her last meeting finished late, and then her taxi got stuck in heavy traffic. When she finally ran into the airport, the screen said "Gate closed". She had missed her flight. "There are no more flights to London tonight," the woman at the desk said. "The next one is tomorrow at eleven."

Anna sat down on a bench and almost cried. Then she looked at her phone. There was a night train from Vienna that went across Europe. If she took it, she could change trains in the morning and arrive in London by the afternoon. It was not perfect, but it was better than nothing. She bought a ticket and took a taxi to the station.

The train was old but comfortable. Anna's ticket was for a small compartment with four seats which became beds at night. When she opened the door, there was already one passenger inside. He was an old man with white hair and a thick grey jumper. He was drawing something in a notebook.

"Good evening," he said with a warm smile. "I'm Henry. It seems that we are travelling together tonight."

Anna said hello and sat down. She opened her laptop immediately. She wanted to prepare for her meeting. But the Wi-Fi on the train didn't work, and after twenty minutes her battery was almost empty. She closed the laptop and sighed.

"Bad day?" asked Henry.

"Terrible," said Anna. She told him about the traffic and the missed flight. "And now I'm going to be late for a very important meeting."

Henry nodded. "I missed a train once, fifty years ago," he said. "It was the best thing that ever happened to me."

Anna looked at him with surprise. "Why?"

"I was twenty-two and I was going to Paris for a job interview," he explained. "I had planned everything. But I missed my train, so I had to wait in a small town in Switzerland for a whole day. I went for a walk by a lake, and I met a young woman who was painting the mountains. Her name was Maria. I never went to the interview. A year later, we got married."

Anna smiled for the first time that day. "Is she travelling with you?"

Henry's face became a little sad. "She died two years ago. We had forty-seven happy years together. Now I travel to the places we used to visit. I draw them in this notebook. It helps me remember her."

He showed her the notebook. It was full of beautiful drawings: bridges, mountains, old churches and small cafés. Under each drawing, he had written the name of the place and a short memory. Anna turned the pages slowly.

They talked for hours. The train was moving through the dark, and from time to time Anna saw the lights of small villages through the window. Henry asked her about her life. She told him about her job, her long hours and her small flat that she hardly ever saw in daylight.

"And what do you love doing?" he asked.

Anna opened her mouth, but she didn't know what to say. "I used to love photography," she said at last. "When I was at university, I took photos everywhere. I even won a small competition once. But I haven't taken a real photo for years. I don't have time."

Henry looked at her kindly. "If you don't make time for the things you love, nobody will make it for you," he said. "Believe me. Time goes faster than you think."

That night Anna lay in her narrow bed and couldn't sleep. She kept thinking about Henry's words. She remembered the old camera at the back of her wardrobe. She remembered how happy she had been when she was walking around with it, looking for the perfect picture.

Early in the morning, she woke up and looked out of the window. The train was passing through the mountains. The sun was rising, and the snow on the tops of the mountains was pink and gold. It was the most beautiful thing she had seen for a long time. She took a photo with her phone, then another and another.

Henry was awake, too. "Ah," he said. "Now you have the right face. That's the face of a photographer."

At the next station, Anna's phone found a signal at last. There was an email from her client. He said that he was ill and the meeting had been moved to Wednesday. Anna laughed out loud. All that stress, and the meeting wasn't even happening.

She thought for a moment. Then she wrote an email to her boss. She said that she would take a few days of holiday. She hadn't had one for two years, and she knew her boss couldn't say no.

"Henry," she said, "where are you going next?"

"To a little town by a lake in Switzerland," he said, smiling. "The place where I met Maria. I go there every spring."

Anna didn't change trains for London. Instead, she got off with Henry in Switzerland. She bought a cheap camera in a shop near the station. Then she spent three days walking by the lake and taking photos of boats, flowers, old houses and people's faces. Henry showed her his favourite places, and in the evenings they ate soup in a small café and talked.

When she finally went back to London, she felt different. She still worked hard, but she stopped answering emails after seven o'clock. Every Sunday she went out with her camera. In the summer, she sent Henry a letter with her best photo inside. It showed an old man drawing in a notebook by a lake.

Under the photo she wrote: "Thank you for the missed flight."
