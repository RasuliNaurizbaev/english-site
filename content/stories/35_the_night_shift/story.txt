The Night Shift

Chloe Bennett had been a nurse for exactly seven months, and tonight was only her fourth night shift in the emergency department of St Mary's Hospital in Leeds. She arrived at a quarter to eight in the evening, put on her blue uniform and tied her hair back. Her hands were already a little cold, which always happened when she was nervous.

Before she had started nursing, Chloe used to imagine that hospitals were calm places, where doctors walked slowly along clean corridors. The emergency department was nothing like that. It was loud, bright and always full. People were brought in by ambulance, or they arrived by themselves, holding their arms or their heads. Some were badly hurt, and others had simply nowhere else to go.

The nurse in charge that night was Maria, a Portuguese woman in her fifties who had been working in the department for over twenty years. Maria was famous for two things: she never panicked, and she remembered everyone's name. "Stay close to me tonight," she told Chloe. "If you're not sure about something, ask. Nobody will think you're stupid. It's much worse to guess."

The first few hours were busy but not too difficult. Chloe cleaned and dressed a cut on a young man's hand, which he had injured while cooking. She took the blood pressure of an elderly woman who had felt dizzy at the supermarket. She helped a little girl who had pushed a small plastic toy up her nose. The girl's father was more frightened than the girl, and Chloe spent ten minutes calming him down.

At around eleven o'clock, a man in his sixties was brought in. His name was Mr Hughes, and he had been feeling pain in his chest since dinner. His wife had made him come, although he kept saying it was only indigestion. Chloe attached him to the heart monitor and took some blood. Mr Hughes was polite and even made jokes, but Chloe could see that he was scared. She asked him whether he had ever had heart problems before. He said no, but that his father had died of a heart attack at the same age.

The doctor, Dr Osei, looked at the results and decided to keep him in for observation. "He's stable for now," he told Chloe, "but check on him every thirty minutes."

After midnight, the department became much busier. There had been a car accident on the motorway, and three people were being brought in at the same time. Maria quickly told everyone where to go. Chloe was sent to help with a young woman who had broken her leg. The woman was crying with pain and kept asking about her boyfriend, who had been driving the car. Chloe held her hand while the doctor gave her something for the pain. She didn't know where the boyfriend was, but she promised to find out.

For the next two hours, Chloe didn't stop moving. She carried blood samples to the laboratory, brought blankets, answered phones and wrote notes. At one point, a drunk man in the waiting room started shouting at the receptionist, and security had to be called. Chloe had been warned that this happened at night, but it was still frightening to see. She was so busy that she didn't have time to drink even a glass of water.

At half past two, she suddenly remembered Mr Hughes. She realised that she hadn't checked on him for almost an hour. Her stomach went cold. She ran to his bed. He was pale and sweating, and he was holding his chest. "It's getting worse," he whispered. The numbers on the monitor had changed.

For a second, Chloe couldn't move. Then she remembered what Maria had told her. She pressed the emergency button and called for Dr Osei. Within seconds, the team was there. Mr Hughes was having a heart attack. He was given medicine, and the hospital's heart specialist was woken up and called in. Twenty minutes later, Mr Hughes was taken upstairs for an operation to open the blocked blood vessel in his heart.

When it was all over, Chloe went into the small staff kitchen and sat down. Her hands were shaking. She couldn't stop thinking about the hour she had missed. If she had checked on him earlier, the problem would have been noticed sooner. If she had come even ten minutes later, he might have died.

Maria found her there a few minutes later. She made two cups of tea and sat down opposite her. Chloe told her everything. She said that she had forgotten about him and that it was her fault.

Maria listened without interrupting. Then she said, "Chloe, listen to me. Tonight we had three people from a car accident, a drunk man and a waiting room full of patients. There were five of us. You were doing three jobs at once. Yes, you should have checked on him, and next time you will. But when you saw that something was wrong, you didn't panic. You called for help immediately. That's why he's alive."

She told Chloe that in her first year, she had made mistakes too, and that she still remembered every one of them. "The day you stop worrying about your patients," she said, "is the day you should stop being a nurse."

At four o'clock, the department went quiet for the first time that night. Chloe finally had a chance to keep her promise. She called the other hospital where the young driver had been taken and found out that he only had a broken arm. When she told the young woman, she started crying again, but this time with relief.

The last hours of the shift passed slowly. Chloe was so tired that her eyes kept closing. She drank too much coffee and ate a chocolate bar from the machine, which was the only food she had had since the evening. Outside, the sky slowly turned from black to grey and then to pale pink.

At half past seven, just before the end of her shift, the phone rang. It was a nurse from the heart unit upstairs. She said that Mr Hughes's operation had been successful and that he was awake. He had asked who the nurse downstairs had been. He wanted to thank the young woman who had pressed the button.

Chloe went upstairs before she went home. Mr Hughes was lying in bed with a tube in his arm, and his wife was sitting next to him, holding his hand. "That's her," he told his wife, smiling weakly. "That's the one who saved me."

Chloe didn't feel like a hero. She still felt guilty about the missing hour. But she smiled and told him to rest.

When she finally walked out of the hospital at eight o'clock, the city was waking up. People were hurrying to work with coffee and newspapers, and nobody knew what had happened inside the building during the night. Chloe was more tired than she had ever been in her life. But as she walked to the bus stop, she realised that she didn't want to do any other job. Tomorrow night, she would be back.
