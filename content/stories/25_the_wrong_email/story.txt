The Wrong Email

Laura had been working at a software company in Manchester for two years. She worked in the customer support team, which answered questions from people who were having problems with the company's programs. Laura was good at her job. She was patient, polite and always ready to help. But inside, she was becoming more and more unhappy. Her team had too much work, and nobody seemed to notice.

The problem had started six months earlier, when the company had changed its computer system. The new system was slow, and it often crashed. Customers were angry because they had to wait for days for an answer. Laura and her colleagues had been working late almost every evening. Two of them had already left the company, and they had not been replaced. Laura had tried to explain the situation to her manager, Mr Price, several times. Each time, he had listened politely and then said, "We all have to work harder at the moment."

One Thursday evening in November, Laura was still at her desk at half past seven. She had been answering emails since eight in the morning, and she had not had a proper lunch. Her best friend at work, Sophie, had gone home an hour earlier. Laura decided to write her a message to explain how she felt. She wrote quickly, without thinking too much.

"I can't do this any more," she wrote. "Mr Price has no idea what is happening in our team. He sits in his office all day and goes to meetings about 'customer happiness', but he has never spent a single hour answering customers himself. The new system is a disaster, and everybody knows it except the people who bought it. If things don't change soon, I'm going to start looking for another job. Honestly, I sometimes wonder whether anyone upstairs even knows our names."

She pressed "Send", closed her laptop and went home. She felt a little better.

The next morning, Laura was drinking her coffee when her phone started to buzz. It buzzed again, and then again. She had eleven new messages. The first one was from Sophie. It said only, "Laura, CHECK YOUR SENT EMAILS. NOW."

Laura opened her laptop with shaking hands. Her heart almost stopped. She had not sent the message to Sophie. When she had started typing the name, the computer had suggested "Staff - All", the list that was used for company announcements. She had clicked on it without looking. Her email had been sent to all three hundred and forty people in the company, including Mr Price, the directors and the head of the whole company, Mrs Okafor.

For a few minutes, Laura just sat there. She wanted to disappear. She thought about calling in sick. She even thought about never going back to the office at all. "If only I had checked the address," she said to herself again and again. "If I had waited until the morning, none of this would have happened."

But Laura was not the kind of person who ran away from problems. She took a deep breath and called her mother, who had worked as a nurse for thirty years and always gave practical advice. Her mother listened carefully. "You can't change what you wrote," she said. "But you can decide what you do next. Did you write anything that wasn't true?" Laura thought about it. "No," she said. "Some of it was rude, but it was all true." "Then go in, apologise for the way you said it, and don't apologise for what you meant," her mother said.

When Laura walked into the office, the room went quiet. Some people looked at her with sympathy. Others were obviously trying not to laugh. A man from the finance department, whom she had never spoken to before, gave her a thumbs up as she passed. On her desk there was a note from Mr Price. It said that he wanted to see her in his office at ten o'clock.

Laura spent the next hour preparing what she would say. At ten o'clock, she knocked on his door. Mr Price was sitting behind his desk, and he did not look happy. To her surprise, Mrs Okafor was also in the room.

Laura spoke first. She said that she was very sorry. She explained that the email had been meant for a friend, and that she should never have written about Mr Price in that way. "It was unprofessional, and it was unkind," she said. "I'm truly sorry for that." Then she paused. "But I can't say that I'm sorry about the rest of it. Our team is exhausted. We have been asking for help for months. I should have said it differently, but I believe it needed to be said."

Mr Price's face went red. He started to say something, but Mrs Okafor raised her hand. She asked Laura how many hours her team had been working each week. Then she asked how long customers usually had to wait for an answer, and how many people had left the team. Laura answered every question honestly. She had the numbers on her phone, because she had been recording them for weeks. Mrs Okafor wrote everything down.

At the end of the meeting, Mrs Okafor thanked her and told her that she could go back to work. Laura had no idea what was going to happen. For the rest of the day, she was sure that she would be fired.

On Monday morning, everybody in the company received another email. This time it was from Mrs Okafor. She wrote that she had been told about serious problems in the customer support team. She admitted that the directors had not realised how bad the situation had become. She wrote that two new people would be hired for the team immediately, and that the computer system would be checked by an independent expert. She also announced that every manager, including herself, would spend one day a month working in customer support. At the end of the email, she wrote: "Sometimes we learn the most important things in the most unexpected ways."

Laura was not fired. Mr Price was moved to a different department a few weeks later. Before he left, he came to her desk. Laura expected him to be angry, but he was calm. He told her that he had been upset by her email, but that he had thought about it a lot. "I should have listened to you," he said. "If I had spent more time with your team, I would have seen the problems myself." Laura was so surprised that she didn't know what to say. In the end, they shook hands.

Six months later, things were very different. The new system had been fixed, and the team had grown to twelve people. Customers were waiting hours instead of days for an answer. Laura had been promoted to team leader, and she made sure that everyone in her team went home on time.

Sometimes new employees ask her about "the famous email". Laura always laughs and tells them the whole story. She tells them that being honest helped her more than she had expected, but that it would have been much better to say the same things politely and to the right person. And then she gives them one piece of advice. "Always, always check the address before you press Send."
