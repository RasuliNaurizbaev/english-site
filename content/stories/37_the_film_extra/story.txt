The Film Extra

Lena was a twenty-year-old student who was studying history at university. Like most students, she never had enough money. She had been looking for a weekend job for weeks when her flatmate, Chloe, showed her an advertisement online. A film company was looking for extras for a historical film that was being made in their city. The pay was eighty pounds for one day, and lunch was included.

"You love history," Chloe said. "And you might see someone famous. What have you got to lose?"

Lena sent a photo and filled in a short form. She didn't expect to hear anything, but two days later she received an email. She had been chosen to play "a woman at the market" in a scene that was set in 1890. She was asked to arrive at the film set at six o'clock on Saturday morning.

On Saturday, Lena got up at half past four. It was still dark when she arrived at an old square in the centre of town. She hardly recognised it. The shop signs had been replaced with old wooden ones, the street lights had been covered, and the ground had been covered with straw. There were horses, carts and more than a hundred people in old-fashioned clothes.

A young woman with a clipboard told her to go to a big white tent. Inside, Lena was given a long brown dress, a grey shawl and heavy boots that were one size too big. A make-up artist made her face look tired and dirty. "Market women worked hard," she explained. "They didn't look like film stars." Then someone put a basket of potatoes in her arms.

At seven o'clock, the extras were told to wait in another tent. Lena sat down next to an older man called Frank, who had been working as an extra for fifteen years. "Welcome to the film business," he said with a smile. "Ninety per cent of this job is waiting. If you've brought a book, you'll be fine."

Lena hadn't brought a book. She had imagined that filming would be exciting, but nothing happened for hours. They waited while the lights were moved. They waited while the cameras were checked. They waited while it started raining and then stopped. Frank told her stories about the films he had been in. He had once been a soldier, a waiter, a passenger on a ship and a dead body. "The dead body was the best," he said. "I was lying down for the whole day."

At last, at eleven o'clock, the extras were called to the square. The assistant director, a man with a loud voice, explained the scene. A rich young man would ride into the market on a horse, get off and speak to a flower seller. The extras had to walk around, buy and sell things and look natural. "And please," he shouted, "don't look at the camera!"

Then everyone became excited, because the main actor arrived. It was James Morland, who was one of the most famous actors in the country. Lena had seen all his films. He was shorter than she had expected, but he had a warm smile. Between takes, he chatted with the crew and drank tea from a paper cup, just like everyone else.

They filmed the scene again and again. Each time, Lena had to walk from a fruit stall to the fountain with her basket. Sometimes the horse moved too early. Sometimes a plane flew over the square, and they had to stop. Once, James Morland forgot his words and laughed. By the ninth take, Lena's feet hurt in the big boots, and her arms ached from carrying the potatoes.

Then it happened. On the tenth take, just as James Morland was getting off his horse, Lena's boot caught on a stone. She tripped, and her basket fell. Potatoes rolled all over the ground, and some of them rolled right under the horse's feet. The horse moved back nervously. Lena froze. She was sure that she had ruined the whole scene.

But James Morland didn't stop acting. He bent down, picked up a potato and gave it back to her with a little bow. "I believe this is yours, madam," he said in his character's voice. Then he helped her pick up the rest, before he walked on to the flower seller as if nothing had happened.

"Cut!" shouted the director. There was a long silence. Lena wished the ground would open and swallow her. She was certain that she would be sent home immediately.

Instead, the director walked over with a big smile. "That was wonderful," she said. "It looked completely real. Can we do it again, exactly the same way?"

Lena couldn't believe it. She asked whether the director really wanted her to drop the basket on purpose. The director said that she did. They filmed the scene four more times, and each time Lena dropped her potatoes and James Morland helped her. Afterwards, he came over and shook her hand. He told her that the best moments in films were often accidents. "If you hadn't tripped, the scene would have been boring," he said.

The rest of the day was quieter. Lena and the other extras waited again, ate soup and sandwiches in the tent, and walked through a few more scenes in the background. When they finished at eight o'clock in the evening, she was so tired that she fell asleep on the bus home. She told Chloe the whole story, but she warned her not to get too excited. "Lots of scenes are cut from the final film," Frank had explained. "Don't tell your family until you see it."

Lena followed his advice. She went back to her studies and almost forgot about the film. More than a year later, it came out in cinemas. Lena went to see it with Chloe and a group of friends. She sat in the dark with her heart beating fast, waiting for the market scene.

When it came, it lasted only twelve seconds. But there she was, in her brown dress, dropping her potatoes in front of James Morland. He picked one up, smiled at her and said his line. The audience laughed warmly. Chloe grabbed her arm and screamed so loudly that the people in front turned round.

The film was a big success. In one interview, James Morland was asked about his favourite moment during the filming. He said that it was the scene with the potatoes, which had not been planned at all. He explained that a young extra had tripped and that they had decided to keep it. Lena read the interview five times.

She never became an actress, and she never wanted to. But she still does extra work sometimes, usually when she needs money for a holiday. She always brings a thick book, comfortable socks and plenty of patience. And whenever someone asks her how she can bear the long hours of waiting, she smiles and tells them about the potatoes.
