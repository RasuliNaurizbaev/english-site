The Old Bookshop

Emma was twenty-nine and lived in a small flat in London, where she worked as an accountant for a big insurance company. She had a good salary, but she spent most of her days looking at numbers on a screen. One rainy Tuesday in October, she received a phone call from a lawyer. Her great-aunt Margaret had died, and she had left Emma something in her will: an old bookshop in a seaside town called Hollow Bay.

Emma had not visited Aunt Margaret for nearly fifteen years. When she was a child, she used to spend every summer with her. She would sit for hours in the corner of the shop, reading adventure stories, while her aunt served customers and told her the names of all the authors. Then Emma had grown up, gone to university and become busy. She had always intended to visit again, but she had never found the time. Now it was too late, and she felt guilty.

The following weekend, she took the train to Hollow Bay. The shop was in a narrow street near the harbour. It was smaller than she had remembered. The paint on the door was coming off, and the sign above the window was so faded that it was hard to read the name: "Margaret's Books". Inside, it smelled of old paper and dust. There were books everywhere, on the shelves, on the floor and in boxes on the stairs.

An elderly man was sitting behind the counter, reading a newspaper. He stood up when she came in. "You must be Emma," he said. "I'm Arthur. I've been helping your aunt here for twenty years. She told me you would come." He made her a cup of tea in the tiny kitchen at the back and showed her the accounts. Emma looked at them with the eyes of an accountant, and her heart sank. The shop had been losing money for years. Most months, fewer than a hundred books were sold.

"Why did she keep it open?" Emma asked. Arthur smiled sadly. "Because she loved it. And because the town needed it. Children used to come here after school. Old people came here to talk. It was more than a shop for her." He paused. "But I won't lie to you. Unless something changes, it will close within a year."

On Monday, a man from a property company called Emma. He had heard that she had inherited the building, and he offered her a large amount of money for it. His company wanted to turn the shop into a holiday flat. Emma did the calculations on the train back to London. With that money, she could pay off most of her loan for her flat. It was the sensible thing to do. She told the man that she would give him an answer in two weeks.

But she couldn't stop thinking about the shop. At night she dreamed of the corner where she used to read. At work she found herself searching online for information about small bookshops that had survived. She read about shops that had been saved by organising events, opening cafés or selling books online. One evening she called her friend Leila, who ran a small bakery. "Am I crazy?" Emma asked. "I'm an accountant. I don't know how to run a shop."

"You know more than you think," Leila said. "You understand money, and you love books. If I were you, I would at least try. You can always sell it later. But if you sell it now, you can never get it back." Emma thought about those words for a long time.

The next Friday, she asked her manager whether she could work from home for three months. To her surprise, he agreed, as long as she came to the office once a month. Emma packed two suitcases and moved into the small flat above the bookshop. Arthur was so happy that he almost cried.

The first weeks were very hard. Emma got up early to do her accounting work and spent the afternoons and evenings in the shop. She and Arthur sorted thousands of books. They threw away the ones that had been damaged by water and put the rest in order. Emma painted the door bright blue and had the sign repaired. She set up a website and started selling rare books online, which brought in some money immediately. Arthur, who had never used a computer, was amazed. "Your aunt would have loved this," he said.

Then Emma had another idea. She remembered how much she had enjoyed listening to her aunt as a child. She put an advertisement in the local newspaper for a story hour every Saturday morning. On the first Saturday, only three children came. Emma was disappointed, but she read to them anyway. The next week, there were nine. By December, the shop was so full on Saturday mornings that some parents had to wait outside.

Other people started to come too. A retired teacher asked whether she could run a book club in the evenings. A group of teenagers asked if they could use the back room to write and draw. Emma bought a coffee machine and some second-hand armchairs. Slowly, the shop came back to life. People who had not been inside for years told her stories about Aunt Margaret, and Emma learned things about her aunt that she had never known.

However, by the end of the three months, the numbers were still not good enough. The shop was losing less money than before, but it was still losing money. The man from the property company called again. He said that his offer would only be valid for one more week. Emma sat in the empty shop that evening and cried. She wished she had more time. She wished she had come back while her aunt was still alive.

On Saturday morning, after the story hour, a woman with a little boy waited until everyone had gone. She introduced herself as Mrs Collins, the head of the local school. She told Emma that the school library had been closed because there was no money for it. "My son has started reading because of you," she said. "Would you be interested in working with the school? We have a small budget from the town council for reading programmes. It isn't much, but we could pay you to run classes here twice a week."

That was not all. The teenagers had secretly started an online campaign called "Save Margaret's Books". In just a few days, more than a thousand people had signed it, and some of them had sent small donations. A journalist from a national newspaper wrote an article about the shop, and online orders tripled in a week.

Emma called the property company and told them that the shop was not for sale. Then she went back to London, spoke to her manager and asked to work part-time. He wasn't happy, but he accepted her decision.

A year later, Margaret's Books was not rich, but it was paying for itself. Emma still did some accounting work three days a week, and Arthur still made tea in the tiny kitchen. Every Saturday morning, Emma sat in the corner where she had read as a child and opened a book for a room full of children. Above the counter, she had hung a photo of her aunt. Sometimes, when the shop was quiet, Emma looked at it and said, "Thank you." She was sure that, wherever she was, Aunt Margaret was smiling.
