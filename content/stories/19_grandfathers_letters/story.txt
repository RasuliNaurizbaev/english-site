Grandfather's Letters

Daniel was twenty-four when his grandfather, Walter, decided to move into a smaller flat. The old family house had become too big for him, and the stairs were too difficult for his knees. Daniel offered to help him with the move.

On the second day, Grandfather Walter asked him to clean the attic. "I haven't been up there for ten years," he said. "Throw away anything that is broken. If you find something interesting, bring it down and show me."

The attic was dark and full of dust. There were old chairs, boxes of books and a broken lamp. Daniel worked for hours. He was about to finish when he noticed a small wooden box in the corner, under an old coat. It was locked, but the key was tied to the handle with a piece of string.

Inside the box, there were about fifty letters. The paper was yellow and thin, and the envelopes had old stamps from Canada. They were all addressed to the same person: Miss Rose Baker, 14 Mill Street, Bristol. Daniel didn't know anyone called Rose Baker. His grandmother's name had been Margaret.

He took the box downstairs. His grandfather was sitting in the kitchen with a cup of tea. When he saw the box, his face changed. For a long moment he said nothing.

"Where did you find that?" he asked quietly.

"In the attic, under an old coat. I'm sorry, Grandad. Should I put it back?"

Walter shook his head slowly. "No. Sit down. I think it's time you heard this story."

He picked up the first letter and touched it gently. "When I was nineteen," he began, "I used to work at a bakery in Bristol. I got up at four every morning and made bread. Rose was the baker's daughter. She used to sing while she worked, and she laughed at all my bad jokes. We fell in love that summer."

Daniel listened in surprise. He had never imagined his quiet, serious grandfather as a young man in love.

"But we were very poor," Walter went on. "My father had lost his job, and my family needed money. A friend told me that there was work in Canada, in the forests. The pay was good. So I decided to go for two years. I promised Rose that I would come back and marry her. I wrote to her every week."

"But these letters are here," said Daniel. "Didn't she get them?"

"Read one," said his grandfather.

Daniel opened a letter carefully. The handwriting was neat and a little old-fashioned. "My dear Rose," it began. "Today it snowed again. The forest is so quiet that I can hear my own heart. I work from morning until night, and I am always tired, but I think of you every evening. I have already saved enough money for a small house. Wait for me, my love." On the back of the envelope there was a stamp: "Returned to sender. Address unknown."

Daniel looked at the other envelopes. Every one had the same stamp.

"I didn't understand," said Walter. "Week after week, my letters came back. I thought that Rose had forgotten me, or that she had found someone else. I was heartbroken. When I came home two years later, I went straight to Mill Street. But the bakery had closed, and the family had moved away. Nobody knew where they had gone."

"Did you ever find her?" Daniel asked.

"Not for a long time," said Walter. "I looked for her for a year. Then I met your grandmother, Margaret. She was kind and clever, and she made me happy. We had a good life together, and I loved her very much. But I never threw away these letters."

Daniel was silent. He could see that his grandfather's eyes were wet.

Then Walter smiled a little. "But the story isn't finished," he said. "About five years ago, after your grandmother died, I got a phone call from a woman. She said that her name was Helen and that she was Rose's daughter. Rose had died, and Helen had found my name in her mother's diary."

"What did the diary say?" asked Daniel.

"It explained everything. Rose's father had had serious money problems. The bakery was sold, and the family had to leave Bristol very quickly. They moved to Scotland. Rose had written to me many times, but she had sent her letters to the wrong address in Canada. The company had moved me to another camp, and nobody had sent my post on. We had both been writing, and we had both been waiting. We just never found each other."

Daniel didn't know what to say. "That's so sad," he said at last.

"Yes," said Walter. "But Helen told me something else. Rose had a happy life too. She married a good man and had three children. And on her last birthday, she told Helen about a boy from the bakery who used to make her laugh. She said that she had never forgotten him."

They sat together in the quiet kitchen for a long time. Outside, it was getting dark.

That evening, Daniel read all the letters, one by one. In them, he met a young man he had never known. This young man was brave, funny and full of dreams. He wrote about bears in the forest, the cold Canadian winter and the friends he had made at the camp. He described the house he wanted to build, with a garden full of roses.

Before Daniel went home, he asked his grandfather a question. "Grandad, can I keep the letters? I don't want them to be lost again."

Walter thought for a moment, then nodded. "Yes. Take them. But promise me one thing. If you ever love someone, tell them clearly, and don't wait too long. Life doesn't always give you a second chance."

A few months later, Daniel visited his grandfather in his new flat. He brought a present. He had scanned all the letters and made them into a beautiful book with a dark blue cover. On the first page, he had written: "The Letters of Walter Evans, 1958 to 1960. A love story."

His grandfather held the book for a long time. Then he looked up and smiled. "You know," he said, "if I hadn't gone to Canada, you wouldn't be here. Life is strange like that."

Daniel smiled back. Later, the blue book stood on the shelf next to a photo of his grandmother, Margaret.
