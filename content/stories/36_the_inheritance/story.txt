The Inheritance

Tom Harper was twenty-nine and lived in a small rented flat above a noisy café in the city. He worked in a call centre, where he spent eight hours a day answering complaints about broken washing machines. He had been doing this job for four years, and he had stopped expecting anything exciting to happen to him.

One Tuesday morning, a thick envelope arrived. It had been sent by a lawyer called Mr Fletcher, and it was about someone Tom hardly remembered: his great-aunt Edith. She had died three weeks earlier, at the age of eighty-seven. Tom read the letter twice before he understood it. Aunt Edith had left him her house in Ashcombe, a tiny village in the hills, two hundred kilometres away.

Tom phoned his mother immediately. "Why me?" he asked. "I only met her once, when I was eight."

His mother was just as surprised. "She was always a bit unusual," she said. "She never married, and she didn't like visitors. Your father used to say that she preferred her bees to people."

A few days later, Tom took the train to meet Mr Fletcher. The lawyer was a tall, quiet man whose office was full of old books. He explained that the house was in good condition and that the garden was large. Then he cleared his throat.

"There is one condition in your aunt's will," he said. "You may only keep the house if you live in it for a full year. You must also look after her bees. If you leave before the year is over, the house will be given to the village, and it will be turned into a library."

Tom laughed nervously. "Bees? I don't know anything about bees. I've never even had a pet."

Mr Fletcher smiled politely. "Your aunt said you would ask that. She told me to give you this." He handed Tom a small notebook with a green cover. On the first page, in careful handwriting, Edith had written: "Everything you need to know about bees. Don't be afraid of them. They can tell when you are."

On the train home, Tom thought about his choices. If he refused, nothing in his life would change. If he accepted, he would have to leave his job, his friends and the city. He made a list of reasons for and against. The list against was much longer, but he kept thinking about the green notebook.

His best friend, Raj, thought he had gone mad. "You'll be bored in a week," he said. "There's no cinema, no gym, probably no internet. You'll be talking to the bees by Christmas."

"Maybe," Tom replied. "But I wish I had done something brave before I turned thirty."

In March, Tom handed in his notice and moved to Ashcombe. The house was made of grey stone, and it had a red door and a garden that went down to a small river. At the end of the garden stood six white beehives. On the first night, the silence was so deep that he couldn't sleep. He kept waiting for the sound of traffic.

The first weeks were hard. The heating didn't work properly, the nearest supermarket was twenty minutes away by car, and the bees seemed to hate him. The first time he opened a hive, he was stung three times. He had forgotten to put on his gloves, even though the notebook had told him to do it. He sat in the kitchen with ice on his hand and nearly called Raj to say he was coming back.

Then there was a knock at the door. An old man with a white beard was standing outside with a basket of eggs. His name was George, and he had been Edith's neighbour for forty years. "I heard you met the bees," he said with a smile. "Edith was stung all the time when she started. She used to say it was the bees' way of saying hello."

George became Tom's first friend in the village. He showed him how to move slowly near the hives and how to check whether the queen was healthy. He also told him stories about Edith. She had been a science teacher in the village school for thirty years. Every child in Ashcombe had been taught about bees in her garden. "Half the village learned to read in her kitchen," George said. "She was strict, but she was kind."

Slowly, Tom began to enjoy his new life. He found a part-time job at the village shop, where he soon knew everyone by name. In the evenings he read Edith's notebook and tried to follow her advice. By summer, the bees no longer frightened him. He even started to look forward to the mornings when he worked with them.

In July, Tom took his first honey from the hives. It was dark gold and tasted of flowers. He put it in small jars and gave one to everyone who had helped him. Mrs Price from the post office asked if he would sell some at the village market. Within an hour, all forty jars had been sold.

One rainy afternoon in October, Tom was cleaning the bookshelves in the living room when an envelope fell out of an old dictionary. His name was written on it. Inside was a letter from Edith, which had been written two years before she died.

"Dear Tom," it said. "You probably don't remember me, but I remember you. When you were eight, your parents brought you here for one afternoon. Everyone else stayed inside, but you spent two hours in the garden watching the bees. Before you left, you asked me whether I was ever lonely. I told you I wasn't, because I had the bees and the village. You said that when you grew up, you wanted to live in a place like this. I have never forgotten it."

The letter continued: "I don't know what kind of man you have become. Perhaps you are very happy in the city. If so, give the house to the village and don't feel guilty. But if you have stayed until you found this letter, then perhaps the boy in the garden was right."

Tom read the letter three times. Then he sat down at the kitchen table and cried for the first time in years. He didn't remember that afternoon at all, but he knew that she had been right.

In March, exactly one year after he had arrived, Tom visited Mr Fletcher again. The lawyer asked him if he still wanted to sell the house. Tom said that he had no intention of selling it. "I've got bees to look after," he explained.

He did not forget the village, though. He thought that if Edith had still been alive, she would have liked the idea of a library. So he turned the large room at the front of the house into a small reading room for children. Every Saturday, the children of Ashcombe came to borrow books. Afterwards, wearing white suits that were much too big for them, they followed Tom down the garden to visit the bees.
