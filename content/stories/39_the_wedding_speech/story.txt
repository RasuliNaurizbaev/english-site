The Wedding Speech

Oliver Grant was thirty-two and worked as an accountant in a quiet office. He liked numbers because they were clear and honest. He did not like crowds, parties or speaking in public. At school, he used to pretend to be ill whenever he had to give a presentation. Even now, he felt nervous when he had to speak in a meeting with more than five people.

His younger brother, Ben, was the opposite. Ben was loud, funny and popular, and he made friends wherever he went. So Oliver was very surprised when Ben came to his flat one evening in January and asked him a question.

"Emma and I are getting married in June," Ben said. "I want you to be my best man."

Oliver was pleased and honoured. Then he remembered what the best man had to do. "Does that mean I have to give a speech?" he asked.

Ben laughed. "Of course. That's the best part. Don't worry, it only needs to be five minutes. Just tell some funny stories about me."

Oliver said yes, because he couldn't say no to his brother. But that night he lay awake, imagining a hundred and twenty people staring at him. He wished Ben had chosen one of his friends instead. Ben had dozens of friends who would have loved to speak.

For the first two months, Oliver did nothing. Every time he thought about the speech, he felt sick, so he simply stopped thinking about it. In April, his mother phoned and asked how the speech was going. He told her that it was going well. In fact, he hadn't written a single word.

In May, he finally sat down at his computer. He searched online for "how to write a best-man speech" and found hundreds of websites. Most of them recommended starting with a joke. So Oliver collected jokes. He found jokes about marriage, jokes about brothers and jokes about wedding cake. He put them together into a speech and read it aloud to his cat. The cat left the room.

The next weekend, he practised the speech in front of his friend Nadia, who had been to more weddings than anyone he knew. When he finished, she was quiet for a moment.

"Well?" Oliver asked nervously.

"The jokes are fine," she said carefully. "But they could be about anyone. Where is Ben? Where are you? It doesn't sound like you at all."

Oliver knew she was right. He threw the speech away and started again. This time, he tried to write funny stories about Ben, as his brother had asked. But he wasn't good at telling funny stories. Every story he wrote sounded either boring or unkind.

Two weeks before the wedding, he still had nothing. He had been working on the speech every evening for a month, and he was exhausted. He began to imagine terrible things. What if he forgot everything? What if nobody laughed? What if he ruined the most important day of his brother's life?

One evening, he visited his parents and went up to his old bedroom to look for some photos. At the back of a cupboard, he found a box of things from his childhood. Inside, there was a drawing that Ben had made when he was six. It showed two boys holding hands. Under it, in big untidy letters, Ben had written: "My brother Ollie. He is not scared of the dark when I am."

Oliver sat on the floor and looked at it for a long time. He remembered the nights when Ben, who had been afraid of the dark, used to climb into his bed. He remembered teaching Ben to ride a bike and helping him with his maths homework. He also remembered the year Oliver's first girlfriend had left him, when Ben had driven three hours in the rain to sit with him and watch bad films.

That night, Oliver wrote a completely new speech. There were no jokes from the internet in it. He wrote about the two brothers who were so different, and about how they had looked after each other in different ways. He wrote about the drawing. Then he wrote about Emma, and about the first time Ben had talked about her. Ben had said that she was the only person who made him want to be quiet and listen. The speech took him two hours to write, and it was the easiest thing he had written all year.

On the day of the wedding, the weather was perfect. The ceremony was held in a small church, and the reception took place in a large hall by a lake. Oliver managed to enjoy the ceremony. But during the dinner his hands started shaking, and he couldn't eat anything.

Then the music stopped, and someone handed him a microphone. A hundred and twenty faces turned towards him. He stood up, unfolded his paper and looked at it. For a terrible moment, the words seemed to swim on the page. His mind went completely empty.

He looked up and saw Ben, who was smiling at him and giving him a small nod. Oliver put the paper down on the table.

"I should tell you something," he began. "I'm not very good at speeches. When Ben asked me to do this, I wanted to run away. I've been worried about this moment for six months." People laughed kindly, and he felt a little better.

Then he told them about the drawing. He took it out of his pocket and held it up. He told them how his little brother had always believed he was brave, even when he wasn't. "The truth is that Ben has always been the brave one," he said. "He talks to strangers, he tries new things, and he isn't afraid to show what he feels. I've spent my whole life learning from him."

He talked about Emma, and he repeated what Ben had said about her. He saw Emma wipe her eyes. Then he raised his glass. "If someone had told me twenty years ago that this noisy little boy would find someone so perfect for him, I wouldn't have believed it. To Ben and Emma."

There was a moment of silence. Then everyone stood up and applauded. Ben came over and hugged him for a long time. "You told me you couldn't do speeches," he said quietly. "That was the best speech I've ever heard."

Later that evening, several guests came to talk to Oliver. An old aunt told him that she hadn't cried at a wedding for forty years. A young man asked whether he could copy the idea for his own sister's wedding. Even Nadia admitted that she had cried.

Oliver still doesn't enjoy speaking in public. But he learned something important at his brother's wedding. People don't need you to be perfect or funny. They need you to be honest. And Ben's drawing is now in a frame on his living room wall.
