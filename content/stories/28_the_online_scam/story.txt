The Online Scam

Harold Green was eighty-one years old, and he had lived alone since his wife, Mary, had died four years earlier. He lived in a small house with a garden full of roses, which Mary had planted many years before. Harold had worked as a train driver for forty years. He was careful with money, and over his long life he had saved nearly thirty thousand pounds. He used to say that this money was for his granddaughter, Lucy, and for "a rainy day".

Lucy was twenty-five and worked as a nurse in a hospital across town. She visited her grandfather every Sunday. Two years earlier, she had bought him a tablet and taught him how to use it. At first Harold had refused to touch it. But slowly he had started to enjoy it. He read the news, looked at photos of trains and sent Lucy short messages with too many full stops.

One Tuesday afternoon, the phone rang. A polite young man introduced himself as Daniel from the fraud department of Harold's bank. He knew Harold's full name and address, and he even knew the name of his bank. "I'm afraid there is a problem, Mr Green," he said. "Someone has been trying to take money from your account. We think criminals have stolen your details."

Harold felt his stomach go cold. He asked what he should do. Daniel explained that the bank needed to move his money to a new, safe account as quickly as possible. "Don't worry," he said in a calm voice. "We do this every day. I'll help you step by step."

Daniel told Harold to open his tablet and download a small program. He said it would allow the bank to "check the computer for viruses". Harold did what he was told. Then Daniel asked him to open his online banking. "Please don't tell anyone about this call," Daniel added. "The criminals may be watching. Even your family might be in danger if they know."

For the next hour, Daniel guided him through everything. Harold had to read out a code that had been sent to his phone. Daniel said this was a normal security check. Then a message appeared on the screen. It said that ten thousand pounds would be transferred to the new account. Harold hesitated. "Ten thousand?" he asked. "Is that right?"

"That's only the first part," Daniel said. "We'll move the rest tomorrow. You're doing very well, Mr Green."

Harold's hand was shaking. Something didn't feel right, but Daniel sounded so kind and so professional. He was just about to press the button when he heard a knock at the door. It was his neighbour, Mrs Patel, who had brought him some soup. Harold told Daniel he would call him back, and he put the phone down.

Mrs Patel noticed immediately that he looked pale. When she asked what was wrong, Harold remembered that he had promised not to tell anyone. But he was too worried to keep quiet. He told her everything. Mrs Patel listened and then said firmly, "Harold, call Lucy. Right now."

Lucy was on her break at the hospital when her phone rang. As soon as she heard the words "safe account", she knew. "Grandad, don't press anything," she said. "Turn the tablet off. That man wasn't from the bank. It's a scam."

She left work early and drove straight to his house. Harold was sitting at the kitchen table, looking much older than usual. Lucy called the real bank using the number on the back of his card. The woman she spoke to confirmed that the bank had never called him. She explained that a bank would never ask a customer to move money to another account, and it would never ask for security codes on the phone. Harold's account was blocked for a few days to protect it, and his money was safe. Not a single pound had been lost.

But Harold didn't feel lucky. He felt stupid and ashamed. "I drove trains for forty years," he said quietly. "Thousands of people trusted me with their lives. And I nearly gave all my money to a stranger on the phone. If Mrs Patel hadn't knocked on the door, I would have lost everything. How could I be so foolish?"

Lucy held his hand. She told him that these criminals were very clever. They had stolen information from somewhere, so they had sounded real. "They don't only call old people," she said. "Last year a doctor I work with lost five thousand pounds in the same way. She's thirty-five and very smart. These people are professionals. You did nothing wrong by believing them. You did the right thing by stopping."

They reported the call to the police. A young police officer came the next day and asked Harold a lot of questions. She asked him what the man had sounded like and whether he had given his real name. She also asked how much information the caller had known. Then she told them something surprising. In the past month, more than forty similar calls had been reported in their part of town. Most of the victims were over seventy, and many of them had been too embarrassed to tell their families.

That evening, Lucy found her grandfather sitting quietly at the table. "Forty people," he said. "I wish someone had warned me. If I had known how these people work, I would have put the phone down at once." Then he looked up at her. "Maybe we could warn the others."

It was his idea, but Lucy helped to organise it. They asked the manager of the local community centre if they could use a room. Lucy designed a simple poster on her computer, and Harold put copies in the library, the post office and the chemist's. The poster said: "Has someone called you about your money? Come and talk. Free tea and biscuits."

On the first Thursday, Lucy expected five or six people. Thirty-two people came. Harold stood at the front, nervous at first, and told his story honestly. He didn't hide how close he had come to losing his savings. When he finished, the room was quiet. Then an old woman in the second row put her hand up. She said the same thing had happened to her husband, but he had never told anyone because he felt so ashamed. One by one, other people began to share their stories.

Lucy then explained a few simple rules. Never give security codes to anyone. Never download a program because a stranger tells you to. If someone says your money is in danger, put the phone down and call your bank yourself, using a number you trust. And if someone tells you to keep a secret from your family, that is the clearest warning of all.

The meetings became a regular event, on the first Thursday of every month. A local newspaper wrote an article about them, with a photo of Harold holding a cup of tea. The headline was "Former Train Driver Helps Neighbours Stay on the Right Track". Harold pretended to be embarrassed, but Lucy noticed that he had cut out the article and put it on the fridge.

Now, whenever Harold's phone rings and he doesn't know the number, he smiles and says, "Sorry, I never discuss money on the phone. Goodbye." Then he sends Lucy a message, with far too many full stops, to tell her that another one has tried and failed.
