Ten Years Later

On their last day at school, five friends had sat on the grass behind the sports hall and made a promise. Whatever happened, they would meet again in exactly ten years, at the same Italian restaurant where they had celebrated their exam results. Each of them had written their plans for the future on a piece of paper. The papers had been put in an envelope, and the envelope had been given to Hannah, who was the most organised of them.

Ten years later, Hannah found the envelope in a drawer while she was moving house. She had almost forgotten about it. She looked at the date on it and realised that the anniversary was only three weeks away. That evening, she sent a message to the other four. She wasn't sure that anyone would come.

To her surprise, everybody replied within a day. Jack was living in London, Sofia was in Spain, Marcus had stayed in their hometown, and nobody had heard from Ella for years. Ella was the last to answer, but she wrote: "I wouldn't miss it for anything."

On the evening of the dinner, Hannah arrived first. The restaurant had been painted and the menus had been changed, but the old owner, Mr Rossi, was still there. He remembered her immediately. "The five students who sang so loudly!" he said. "You were all so young."

Marcus came next. At school, he had been the quiet one who loved computers. Now he was wearing a smart jacket and carrying a car key in his hand. Then Sofia arrived with a big smile and a tan, and Jack came in late, still talking on his phone. Ella was the last. Her hair was short now, and she looked tired, but she hugged everyone warmly.

At first, the conversation was a little awkward. They talked about the weather, the traffic and the food. But after the first bottle of wine, they began to relax. They laughed about their old teachers and the time Jack had accidentally set fire to the science lab. Soon it felt as if they had never been apart.

After dinner, Hannah took out the envelope. "Do you remember these?" she asked. Everyone groaned and laughed. They agreed that each person would read their own paper aloud.

Jack went first. At eighteen, he had written that he would be a famous musician, living in Los Angeles. He read it and laughed. "Well, I do live in a big city," he said. In fact, he worked in a bank in London. He had been playing guitar in a band for a few years, but the band had broken up when the other members got married. "I earn good money," he admitted. "But I sometimes wish I had tried harder with the music. If I had moved to America when I was twenty, maybe things would have been different."

Sofia was next. She had planned to become a doctor, marry at twenty-five and have three children. Instead, she had left medical school after two years. "I realised that I hated hospitals," she explained. She had moved to Spain to learn the language and had never come back. Now she ran a small hotel by the sea with her partner. She had no children yet, but she didn't seem to mind. "My life looks nothing like this paper," she said. "And I'm glad."

Marcus unfolded his paper and smiled. He had written that he wanted to leave their town as soon as possible and start his own company. He had started his own company, but he had never left. His software business, which had begun in his parents' garage, now employed thirty people. "I used to think that you had to go somewhere far away to be successful," he said. "I was wrong. Everything I needed was here."

Then it was Hannah's turn. At school, she had always known exactly what she wanted. She had planned to study law, become a lawyer and buy a house by the age of twenty-eight. She had done all of those things. But as she read her paper aloud, her voice became quieter. "I did everything on the list," she said. "And last year, I realised that I wasn't happy. I'd been working twelve hours a day for five years. I didn't have time for anything else." She told them that she had recently left her job. She was moving house because she wanted to start again in a smaller place.

The table went silent. Then Sofia reached over and squeezed her hand. "That's the bravest thing anyone has said tonight," she said.

Finally, everyone looked at Ella. She held her paper for a long time without opening it. When she spoke, her voice was calm. She explained that her mother had become seriously ill a year after school. Ella had given up her place at art college to look after her. For six years, she had worked in a supermarket and cared for her mother in the evenings. Her mother had died two years ago.

"I'm sorry we didn't know," Marcus said quietly. "Why didn't you tell us?"

Ella shrugged. "Everyone was busy with their new lives. I didn't want to be the sad friend." She told them that she had felt embarrassed for years, because she thought she had fallen behind everyone else.

Then she opened her paper. At eighteen, she had written only one sentence: "I want to make things that make people feel something." She looked at it and smiled for the first time that evening. She explained that she had started painting again after her mother's death. Last month, a small gallery in the city had agreed to show her work. It would be her first exhibition.

Everyone cheered so loudly that Mr Rossi came out of the kitchen to see what was happening. When he heard the news, he brought them a bottle of his best wine.

They stayed until the restaurant closed. Outside, on the pavement, they talked about how strange life was. None of them had the life that they had planned at eighteen. Jack wondered aloud whether they would have been happier if everything had gone to plan. Sofia said that she didn't think so. "If I had become a doctor, I would never have found my hotel," she said. Hannah said that she had followed her plan perfectly, and it hadn't made her happy at all.

Before they said goodbye, Ella suggested a new idea. Instead of writing plans for the next ten years, each of them would write one thing that they had learned. They wrote them on paper napkins from the restaurant, and Hannah put them in a new envelope.

Three months later, all five of them were standing together in a small gallery, looking at Ella's paintings. They had already agreed that they would not wait another ten years to meet again.
