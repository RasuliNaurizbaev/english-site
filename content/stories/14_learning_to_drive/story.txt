Learning to Drive

Michael was thirty-five years old, and he had never driven a car. When he was young, he lived in a big city with good buses and trains, so he didn't need one. Later, he was always too busy. But last spring, Michael and his wife, Helen, moved to a small village in the countryside. The nearest supermarket was eight kilometres away, and there was only one bus a day.

"You have to learn to drive," Helen said after their first week in the village. "I can't take you everywhere." She was right. Michael was a bit embarrassed, but he called a local driving school and booked his first lesson.

His instructor was a woman called Mrs Grant. She was about sixty, with short grey hair and very serious eyes. She had been a driving instructor for thirty years. On the first day, she looked at Michael from head to toe. "Have you ever sat behind the wheel of a car?" she asked.

"Only in video games," Michael answered with a nervous laugh.

Mrs Grant didn't laugh. "This is not a game," she said. "A car is a dangerous machine. If you are careless, people can get hurt. Do you understand?"

"Yes," Michael said quietly. He suddenly felt like a schoolboy again.

The first lessons were very difficult. There were so many things to remember at the same time. He had to check the mirrors, use the pedals, change gear, turn the wheel and watch the road. When he tried to start the car, it stopped with a jump. When he tried again, it made a terrible noise. "Gently!" Mrs Grant said. "Your feet are not elephants."

She was very strict. She never said "good" unless something was really good. When Michael forgot to look in the mirror, she wrote it in her small black notebook. When he drove too fast, she told him to stop the car and explained the rules again. Some evenings, Michael came home tired and angry. "She hates me," he told Helen. "Nothing I do is right."

"Maybe she just wants you to be safe," Helen said.

Slowly, things started to get better. After ten lessons, Michael could drive around the village without problems. After twenty, he could drive on the main road. He practised parking in the supermarket car park on Sunday mornings when it was empty. He also studied the rules of the road every evening and passed the theory test on his first try. He was proud of himself.

Finally, Mrs Grant said he was ready for the practical test. It was booked for a Wednesday in October at nine o'clock in the morning, in the nearest town.

On the morning of the test, it was raining heavily. Michael was very nervous. The examiner was a young man with a clipboard who didn't smile at all. They drove out of the test centre, and for the first ten minutes everything went well. Michael checked his mirrors, used his signals and kept to the speed limit.

Then the examiner asked him to turn right at a busy crossroads. Michael was thinking so hard about the turn that he didn't see the traffic lights change. They had already turned red. He drove straight through. A car horn sounded loudly. Michael's heart almost stopped.

Nobody was hurt, but when they returned to the test centre, the examiner gave him the bad news. "I'm sorry," he said. "You drove through a red light. That is a serious mistake. You have failed the test." Michael felt terrible. He had worked so hard, and it had all gone wrong in a few seconds.

He didn't want to talk to anyone that evening. He thought about giving up. "Maybe I'm just not a driver," he said to Helen. "Maybe some people are too old to learn."

The next day, his phone rang. It was Mrs Grant. Michael expected her to be angry, but her voice was calm. "I heard about the test," she said. "Listen to me, Michael. When I took my first test, I failed it too. I hit the side of the road while I was parking. The examiner said I would never be a good driver. Now I have taught more than a thousand people."

Michael was surprised. He had never imagined that Mrs Grant had failed anything.

"You are a careful driver," she continued. "You only made one mistake because you were nervous. If you give up now, you will always wonder. Book another test. We will practise crossroads until you can do them in your sleep."

So that is what they did. For the next six weeks, Michael had two lessons every week. Mrs Grant took him to every difficult crossroads in the area. She also taught him a trick for his nerves: before every turn, he took one slow breath and said quietly to himself, "Mirror, signal, lights." It seemed silly at first, but it worked.

His second test was in December. The roads were cold and wet, and there was a little snow on the fields. This time the examiner was an older woman with a friendly face. Michael took a slow breath before every turn. "Mirror, signal, lights," he whispered. He stopped at every red light. He even parked perfectly between two cars on a narrow street.

Back at the test centre, the examiner looked at her papers for a long moment. Then she smiled. "Congratulations, Mr Brooks," she said. "You have passed."

Michael wanted to shout and jump, but he only said, "Thank you," and shook her hand. When he came out, Mrs Grant was waiting by the door. She didn't say anything at first. Then, for the first time, she smiled. "Good," she said. "Really good."

That weekend, Michael drove Helen to the supermarket in their old red car. On the way home, he took her for a drive along the coast. "You know," Helen said, "I'm proud of you."

Michael smiled and kept his eyes on the road. "Mirror, signal, lights," he said quietly, and he turned towards home.
