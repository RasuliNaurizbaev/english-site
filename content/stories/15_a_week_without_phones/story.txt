A Week Without Phones

It was a grey Monday morning in March, and class 10B was waiting for their geography lesson. Almost everyone was looking at a phone. Some students were watching videos, some were sending messages and one boy was playing a game with the sound on. Nobody noticed when their teacher, Mr Hughes, came into the room.

Mr Hughes stood at the front and waited. After two minutes, he said loudly, "Good morning!" A few students looked up. Most didn't.

He wrote a number on the board: 7 hours 20 minutes. "Last week, I asked you to check how much time you spend on your phones," he said. "This is the average for this class. Every day."

The room went quiet. "That's not so much," said Jake, a tall boy who sat at the back. But then Olivia, a quiet girl near the window, said, "It's almost as long as a school day."

Mr Hughes smiled. "I'd like to suggest an experiment," he said. "For one week, starting next Monday, nobody in this class uses a smartphone. Not at school and not at home. If you need to call your parents, you can use an old phone without the internet. At the end of the week, we'll talk about what happened. But everyone has to agree. If one person says no, we won't do it."

There was a lot of noise. Some students said it was impossible. Jake said it was a crazy idea. But Olivia raised her hand. "I'll do it," she said. Then her friend Mia agreed, and then another student, and another. Finally, only Jake was left. Everyone was looking at him. "Fine," he said with a sigh. "But I think it's a terrible idea."

That week, the students talked to their parents. Most parents loved the idea. Jake's mother was so happy that she offered to do it too.

On the first day of the experiment, everyone gave their smartphones to their parents in the morning. Olivia felt strange on the bus to school. Usually she listened to music and checked social media. Now she just looked out of the window. She noticed things that she had never seen before: a small bakery on the corner, a man walking five dogs at once, a beautiful old church. She had taken this bus for three years.

At school, the breaks were very different. Without phones, the students didn't know what to do with their hands. For the first two days, many of them felt bored and nervous. Jake kept touching his empty pocket. "I feel like I've lost something," he said. Some students worried that they were missing important news or messages from friends in other classes.

But by Wednesday, something had changed. In the lunch break, a group of students started playing cards. Someone had brought an old football, and a match started in the playground. Mia and Olivia sat with two girls they had never really talked to before, and they discovered that they all loved the same books.

Jake had the biggest surprise. On Wednesday afternoon, he was so bored at home that he opened his old guitar case. His grandfather had given him the guitar for his twelfth birthday, but he had stopped playing because he never had time. Now he had lots of time. He played for two hours that evening, and again on Thursday.

There were problems too. On Thursday, Mia got lost on her way to a friend's house because she couldn't use a map app. She had to ask three people for directions. One of them was an old lady who walked with her all the way to the right street. "Young people never ask for help these days," the lady said. "They only look at their phones." Mia laughed and told her about the experiment.

Other things got better. Most students said that they were sleeping much better. They didn't look at screens late at night, so they fell asleep earlier. Several students finished their homework faster. One boy, Sam, read a whole novel in three days. It was the first book he had finished since primary school.

On Friday afternoon, the week ended. Mr Hughes put the chairs in a circle, and everyone talked about their experience. Olivia said that she had felt more relaxed. "I used to check my phone every five minutes," she said. "I didn't even know why. This week I felt calmer."

Mia told the story about the old lady. "If I had had my phone, I would never have met her," she said.

Then Mr Hughes looked at Jake. "And you, Jake? Was it as terrible as you expected?"

Jake was quiet for a moment. Then he picked up something from the floor. It was his guitar. "I've written a song," he said, a little embarrassed. "It's about this week." He played it for the class. It wasn't perfect, but it was good, and everybody clapped loudly at the end.

On Saturday morning, the students got their smartphones back. Most of them were happy to have them again. They had a lot of messages, but nothing really important had happened. Olivia noticed that she didn't feel excited when she turned her phone on. She just felt normal.

The experiment had finished, but some things stayed. The class decided to keep a new rule: no phones during lunch breaks. Olivia stopped using her phone on the bus and kept looking out of the window. Mia started asking people for directions sometimes, just for fun. And Jake played his guitar every day.

A few weeks later, Jake performed his song at the school concert. Before he started, he spoke into the microphone. "This song was written during a week without phones," he said. "So please, everyone, put your phones away and just listen."

And for three minutes, in a hall full of people, nobody looked at a screen.
