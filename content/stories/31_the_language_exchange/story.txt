The Language Exchange

When Dmitry arrived in London, he thought his English was good. He had studied it at school and at university in Yekaterinburg, and for five years he had read technical documents in English almost every day. He was an engineer, and he had been offered a job with a company which designed bridges. On paper, everything was perfect.

The problem was that real people did not speak like technical documents. In his first week, a colleague asked him, "Fancy a cuppa?" Dmitry smiled politely and said nothing, because he had no idea what the question meant. Later he found out that it was simply an offer of a cup of tea. He wished he had asked.

After a month, he realised that he needed to practise speaking with someone who would not laugh at his mistakes. One evening, in the local library, he noticed a small piece of paper on the noticeboard. It said: "Student of Russian looking for a native speaker for a language exchange. One hour English, one hour Russian. Free coffee included!" There was a phone number and a name: Emma.

Dmitry used to be quite shy, and in Russia he would never have called a stranger. But London had already changed him a little. He sent a message, and two days later he was sitting in a café near the library, opposite a young woman with red hair and a big smile.

Emma was twenty, and she was studying Russian and French at university. She had fallen in love with Russian literature after reading a book by Chekhov at school. "I can read quite well," she explained, "but when I try to speak, my brain stops working. Last summer I went to Saint Petersburg for two weeks, and I was too scared to say anything except 'thank you'."

They agreed to meet every Thursday at six. For the first hour they would speak English, and Emma would correct Dmitry. For the second hour they would switch to Russian, and Dmitry would help her.

The first meetings were full of misunderstandings. Once Emma told him that his presentation idea was "quite good". Dmitry felt happy until she explained that, in British English, "quite good" often means "not very good". He was shocked. "So when my manager says my work is quite interesting, he doesn't like it?" Emma laughed. "It depends on his voice," she said. "English people rarely say what they really mean."

Emma had her own problems too. She told Dmitry that she had once tried to buy bread in a Russian shop and had used the polite form of "you" with a cat. Another time she had wanted to say that she was tired, but the word she used meant that she was bored with the person in front of her. "No wonder the man looked offended," she said, covering her face with her hands.

They also talked about culture. Emma asked him why Russians didn't smile at strangers in the street. Dmitry explained that in Russia a smile was something you gave to people you knew. "If you smile at a stranger for no reason, people think something is wrong with you," he said. Emma found this fascinating. In return, she explained why British people apologised all the time, even when someone else had stepped on their foot.

Week after week, the conversations became easier. Dmitry started to understand jokes at work, and he even made one himself during a meeting. Everyone laughed, and he wasn't sure if they were laughing at the joke or at him, but it still felt like a victory. Emma, meanwhile, had stopped translating every sentence in her head before she spoke.

Then, in March, something went wrong. One Thursday, Dmitry waited at the café for forty minutes, but Emma didn't come. She didn't answer his messages either. The next week, the same thing happened. Dmitry felt hurt. He decided that their friendship had only been a polite arrangement for her, and that she had simply lost interest. "If I had known that she would disappear like this," he thought, "I wouldn't have spent so much time preparing for our meetings."

Three weeks later, he received a long message. Emma's grandmother had been taken to hospital after a fall, and Emma had gone home to Leeds to look after her. She had lost her phone on the train and had only just bought a new one. "I'm so sorry," she wrote. "I should have found a way to tell you. Are you still free on Thursdays?"

Dmitry felt ashamed. He had judged her too quickly. He wrote back immediately and asked how her grandmother was. Emma said that she was getting better, but slowly.

When they met again, Emma looked worried. Her oral exam in Russian was in two weeks, and she had missed a lot of classes. The examiner would ask her to speak for ten minutes about a topic and then answer questions. "I'm going to fail," she said. "I've forgotten everything."

Dmitry suggested meeting twice a week instead of once until the exam. They practised for hours. He pretended to be a strict examiner, asking difficult questions about Russian history, food and family traditions. When she made mistakes, he wrote them down and they went through them together at the end. He told her that she should stop apologising for every small error. "Russians will understand you," he said. "They are just happy that you are trying."

At the same time, Dmitry had his own challenge. His team was preparing a presentation for an important client, and his manager had asked him to present the technical part. Dmitry had never spoken in front of a large group in English. So Emma helped him in return. She listened to his presentation again and again, and she showed him how to make his sentences shorter and more natural. "Don't say 'it is necessary to underline the fact that'," she told him. "Just say 'the important thing is'."

The presentation was on a Tuesday, and Emma's exam was on the Wednesday. On Tuesday evening, Dmitry sent her a message: "The client asked me three questions and I understood all of them. My manager said my part was 'really excellent'. Not 'quite good'!" Emma replied with ten smiling faces.

On Wednesday afternoon, Dmitry's phone rang during a meeting. He went out into the corridor to answer it. Emma was shouting so loudly that he had to hold the phone away from his ear. She had passed with one of the highest marks in her year. The examiner had told her that her Russian sounded natural and confident. He had even asked her whether she had lived in Russia.

That Thursday, they met at the café as usual, but this time Emma brought a cake. She had baked it herself, using a recipe from Dmitry's grandmother, which he had translated for her. It wasn't perfect, but Dmitry said it was the best cake he had eaten in London.

"You know," Emma said, "when I put that note on the noticeboard, I only wanted to improve my Russian. I didn't expect to find a friend."

Dmitry smiled, and for once he didn't care whether it was the Russian or the British way of smiling. "Neither did I," he said. "Same time next week?"

"Of course," she answered in Russian, without even thinking about it.
