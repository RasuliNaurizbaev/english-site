Working from Home

For six years, Lucy Morgan had travelled to work every day by train. She was a graphic designer in a busy agency in the centre of London, and the journey took an hour and a quarter each way. She spent those hours standing in crowded carriages, with someone's elbow in her back and someone else's coffee too close to her shirt. Every evening, she arrived home tired and hungry, with no energy left for anything else.

So when her boss, Karen, announced that the agency was going to let some designers work from home permanently, Lucy was the first to put her hand up. "Are you sure?" Karen asked. "Some people find it harder than they expect." Lucy laughed. She couldn't imagine anything better than never having to take that train again.

The first few weeks were wonderful. Lucy turned the small spare room in her flat into an office. She bought a large desk, a comfortable chair and a plant, which she put next to the window. She no longer had to get up at six. Instead, she woke up at half past seven, made herself a proper breakfast and started work at half past eight, still wearing her slippers.

She loved the silence. In the agency, there had always been phones ringing and people chatting, and she had found it difficult to concentrate. At home, she could listen to her own music and work without being interrupted. She finished her projects faster than ever. At lunchtime, she went for a walk in the park near her flat, and in the evenings she had time to cook, read and see her friends. "Why didn't I do this years ago?" she asked herself.

But slowly, without noticing it, things began to change.

It started with small habits. One morning, she decided that there was no point in getting dressed, since nobody would see her. She worked all day in her pyjamas. A few days later, she stopped making breakfast and just ate a biscuit at her desk. The lunchtime walks became shorter, and then they stopped completely, because it was easier to eat a sandwich in front of the computer.

Her working day also became longer. In the office, she had always left at half past five, when everyone else left. At home, there was nobody to tell her when the day was over. Her laptop was always in the next room, and she would often go back "just to check one thing" at nine or ten in the evening. Soon she was answering emails in bed. Her friends invited her out, but she usually said she was too busy.

By the end of the winter, Lucy had stopped looking forward to anything. Some days she didn't speak to a single person except the delivery driver who brought her shopping. She had been working from home for five months, and she couldn't remember the last time she had laughed out loud. She missed the stupid jokes in the office kitchen. She even missed her colleague Ben, who used to sing badly while he worked and who had driven her crazy.

Her work suffered too. She had always been proud of her ideas, but now they seemed boring and tired. In a video meeting in February, Karen asked her why her latest designs looked so similar to her old ones. Lucy didn't know what to say. After the meeting, she sat at her desk for a long time without moving.

The turning point came on a grey Sunday in March. Lucy's sister, Kate, came to visit without warning. When Lucy opened the door, Kate stared at her. Lucy was wearing the same old jumper she had been wearing for three days, and there were dirty cups on every surface of the flat. The plant by the window had died weeks ago, and Lucy hadn't even noticed.

"Lucy, what's happened to you?" Kate asked. "You look terrible. When was the last time you went outside?"

Lucy started to answer, but then, to her own surprise, she burst into tears. She told Kate everything: the loneliness, the long hours, the feeling that her days had no beginning and no end. "I thought I would be so happy," she said. "I wish I had listened to Karen. She warned me that it might be difficult."

Kate made them both a cup of tea and sat down next to her. "Working from home isn't the problem," she said. "The problem is that you've stopped looking after yourself. You need some rules."

They spent the rest of the afternoon writing a plan. Kate, who had worked from home for years as a translator, explained the things that had helped her. That evening, Lucy stuck the list on the wall above her desk.

The rules were simple. She had to get up at the same time every day and get dressed as if she were going to the office. She had to start work at nine and stop at half past five, and after that the door of the spare room had to be closed. She was not allowed to eat lunch at her desk. She had to go outside at least once a day, even if it was raining. And she had to see or speak to another person, not on a screen, at least three times a week.

The first week was hard. Lucy kept wanting to open her laptop in the evening, and more than once she had to stop herself. But slowly the rules started to work. She began to sleep better. She joined a yoga class on Tuesday evenings, where she made friends with a woman called Priya, who also worked from home. They started meeting for coffee on Fridays.

She also spoke to Karen honestly about how she had been feeling. Karen wasn't surprised. She said that several other designers had been having the same problems. Together, they agreed that Lucy would come into the agency every Wednesday. The train journey was still crowded, but now that it was only once a week, Lucy didn't mind it. She even enjoyed hearing Ben sing again, although she would never tell him that.

Something else happened too. Her ideas came back. Walking in the park, sitting in a café or chatting with colleagues gave her the energy she had been missing. In May, a design she had created for a children's book was chosen by a big publishing company, and Karen sent her a message that said simply: "This is the old Lucy. Welcome back."

A year after she had first started working from home, Lucy bought a new plant for her office. This time, she watered it every Monday. Looking at it, she thought about how much she had learned. Freedom was wonderful, she realised, but only if you knew how to use it. If Kate hadn't knocked on her door that Sunday, she might still be sitting in her pyjamas, answering emails at midnight.

Now, at half past five every day, she closes the door of her office, puts on her coat and goes out into the world.
