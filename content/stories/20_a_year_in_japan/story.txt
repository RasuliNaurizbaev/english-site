A Year in Japan

When Kate Morgan finished university, she didn't know what to do next. She had studied history, but she didn't want to work in an office. One evening she saw an advert online: "Teach English in Japan for one year. No experience needed." She had always been interested in Japan. She had read books about it and watched Japanese films, but she had never been to Asia. She applied the same night.

Two months later, she was sitting on a plane to Osaka. She felt excited and nervous at the same time. "What if I don't like it?" she thought. "What if nobody understands me?"

Her new job was at a secondary school in a small town near Osaka. The school had given her a tiny flat near the station. It had one room, a small kitchen and a bathroom with a very complicated toilet. The toilet had more buttons than her phone, and all the instructions were in Japanese. On her first night, she pressed the wrong button and got water all over her clothes. She laughed so much that she forgot how tired she was.

The first few weeks were difficult. Everything was new. In the supermarket, she couldn't read the labels, so she often bought the wrong things. Once she bought a packet of what she thought was sugar, but it was salt. She put it in her coffee and almost spat it out. At the train station, she got lost again and again because the station was so big.

At school, she worked with a Japanese teacher of English called Mr Tanaka. He was polite and serious, and at first Kate found him a little cold. On her first day, she gave him a big friendly hug. He stood very still and his face turned red. Later, a younger teacher called Yuki explained that people in Japan usually bow when they meet. They don't hug colleagues. "Don't worry," said Yuki, smiling. "He will remember it forever, but he isn't angry."

Kate learned many rules like this. She learned that you take off your shoes before you go into someone's house. She learned that you shouldn't eat while you are walking in the street. She learned that people are very quiet on trains and that nobody talks on the phone there. She had never thought about these things before. At home in England, people did things differently.

Sometimes she felt very lonely. In the evenings she sat in her small flat and called her family on video. "I miss you all," she told her mum one night. "I miss real bread and I miss being able to read things." Her mum listened and said, "If it's still this hard after three months, you can come home. But give it a chance."

The turning point came in her third month. One of the students in her class was a quiet boy called Kenji. He never spoke in lessons. He always looked at his desk when Kate asked him a question. The other teachers said that he was shy and that he didn't like English.

One day, Kate noticed that Kenji had drawn a picture in the corner of his notebook. It was a perfect drawing of a famous footballer from England. "Do you like football?" she asked. For the first time, Kenji looked up. "Yes," he said quietly. "Liverpool." Kate smiled. Her father had supported Liverpool all his life.

The next week, Kate brought some English football magazines to class. She made a lesson about sports, and the students had to describe their favourite players. Kenji spoke for two minutes without stopping. His English wasn't perfect, but everyone listened. When he finished, the class clapped. Mr Tanaka looked at Kate and gave her a small nod. Later he told her that it had been an excellent lesson.

After that, things became easier. Kate started taking Japanese lessons twice a week. She learned to read simple words and to order food in restaurants. Yuki became her best friend. They went to hot springs together, visited old temples in Kyoto and ate street food at summer festivals. Yuki's grandmother taught Kate how to make rice balls and how to wear a kimono.

Even Mr Tanaka changed. Or maybe Kate understood him better. One evening, the teachers went out for dinner together. After two glasses of beer, Mr Tanaka started singing old English pop songs in a karaoke bar. He had a terrible voice, but he knew every word. Kate laughed until she cried. "When I was a student," he told her, "I used to dream about visiting London. I never had the chance." Kate promised to show him around if he ever came.

In winter, Kate made her biggest mistake. She was invited to Yuki's family's house for New Year. She wanted to bring a present, so she bought a beautiful set of four white plates. When Yuki's mother opened the box, there was a short, strange silence. Later Yuki explained that the number four sounds like the word for "death" in Japanese. Kate felt terrible, but Yuki's mother just smiled and said, "Now we know you will never forget this rule."

The year went very fast. Suddenly it was spring again, and the cherry trees were covered in pink flowers. Kate's contract was almost finished. The school had asked her to stay for another year, but her family wanted her to come home. She didn't know what to do.

On her last day at school, the students gave her a large card. Every student had written a message in English. Kenji's message was the longest. He wrote that he had decided to study English at university. "Because of you, I am not afraid to speak now," he had written. Kate read it on the train home and cried quietly, like a true Japanese passenger, without making any noise.

In the end, Kate went back to England for the summer. But in September, she returned to Japan for a second year. When people ask her what she learned, she always gives the same answer. "I learned that mistakes are not a problem. If you are kind and you keep trying, people will help you. And I learned that home is not only one place."
