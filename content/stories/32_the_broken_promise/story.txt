The Broken Promise

Mark Turner had always believed that he was a good father. He worked hard, he paid for everything his family needed, and every summer he took them on holiday to Spain. His son, Leo, was nine, and Mark loved him more than anything in the world. The problem was that he rarely had time to show it.

Mark was a manager at a large insurance company in Manchester. Two years earlier, he had been promoted, and since then his working day had become longer and longer. He usually left the house before Leo woke up and came home after Leo had gone to bed. At weekends his phone never stopped ringing. Even at dinner, he would check his emails under the table, while his wife, Helen, looked at him without saying anything.

It hadn't always been like this. When Leo was small, Mark used to take him to the park every Saturday morning. They would feed the ducks, play football and then buy hot chocolate from a little van near the gate. Leo still talked about those mornings, but they seemed like a long time ago now.

In the past year, Mark had missed a lot of important moments. He had missed Leo's first swimming competition because of a meeting with a client. He had missed parents' evening at school because his train had been cancelled on the way back from London. He had even arrived late for Leo's birthday party, when all the cake had already been eaten. Each time, he apologised and promised that it would never happen again. Each time, Leo said, "It's OK, Dad." But Mark noticed that he said it more quietly every time.

One evening in October, Leo was still awake when Mark came home. He was sitting on the stairs in his pyjamas, holding a piece of paper. "Dad, I've been given a part in the school play," he said proudly. "I'm the king! It's on the twelfth of December at six o'clock. Will you come?"

Mark took the paper and read it. "Of course I'll come," he said. "I promise. I wouldn't miss it for anything."

"You promised last time too," Leo said. Then he went upstairs before Mark could answer.

Those words stayed in Mark's head all night. He wrote the date in his diary, in his phone and on the calendar in the kitchen. He told his secretary that he must not be disturbed on the afternoon of the twelfth. He even asked Helen to remind him every week.

For the next two months, Leo practised his lines every evening with his mother. Sometimes, when Mark came home early enough, he could hear Leo's voice from the living room: "I am the king of this land, and I will not be afraid!" Mark would stand in the corridor and listen with a smile, but he never went in, because he always had another phone call to make.

On the morning of the twelfth, Mark woke up with a good feeling. He had planned everything. He would leave the office at four, which would give him plenty of time to drive to the school. But at eleven o'clock, his boss, Mr Graham, called him into his office.

"We have a serious problem," Mr Graham said. "Our biggest client wants to cancel their contract. Their director is flying in from Germany this afternoon. I need you at the meeting. It starts at three and it might go on until late."

Mark felt his stomach turn. He explained that his son's school play was that evening and that he had promised to be there. Mr Graham looked at him in surprise. He asked Mark whether he really understood how important this client was. "If we lose this contract," he said, "people could lose their jobs. Maybe even you."

Mark went back to his desk and sat there for a long time. He thought about all the times he had chosen work over his family. He remembered Leo's face on the stairs. If only he hadn't made so many promises that he couldn't keep. He picked up his phone to send Helen a message, saying that he would be late again. He typed it, read it, and then deleted it.

Then he did something he had never done before. He went back to Mr Graham's office and knocked on the door. "I'm sorry," he said. "I'll prepare everything for the meeting, and Sarah from my team can present it. She knows the client as well as I do. But I'm leaving at four. My son has been waiting for this evening for two months. If I break this promise, I don't think he'll ever believe me again."

Mr Graham was silent for a moment. Mark was sure that he was going to be shouted at. Instead, his boss sighed and looked at a photo on his desk. It showed two teenage girls on a beach. "My daughters are at university now," he said quietly. "I can't remember a single one of their school plays. I wasn't there for any of them." He told Mark to go.

Mark spent the next four hours working harder than he had ever worked. He wrote notes for Sarah, prepared all the documents and called the client to apologise personally. At five past four, he ran out of the building.

The traffic was terrible. It was raining heavily, and there had been an accident on the main road. Mark sat in his car, watching the minutes pass. At ten to six, he was still three kilometres from the school. He left the car in a supermarket car park and ran the rest of the way in the rain, in his expensive office shoes.

When he opened the door of the school hall, the lights had already been turned off and the play had started. He was completely wet, and his hair was stuck to his forehead. He stood at the back, trying to breathe quietly.

A few minutes later, the king came onto the stage. Leo was wearing a golden paper crown and a red cloak which Helen had made from an old curtain. He looked out at the audience, searching for something. Mark knew exactly what he was looking for. He raised his hand and waved.

Leo saw him. For a second he forgot his line, and then the biggest smile Mark had ever seen spread across his face. "I am the king of this land," Leo said, louder and more confidently than ever, "and I will not be afraid!"

After the play, Leo ran across the hall and jumped into his father's arms, even though Mark was still wet. "You came!" he shouted. "You really came!"

"I promised," Mark said, and his voice was not quite steady.

The next day, he found out that Sarah's presentation had gone very well and that the client had decided to stay. Nobody lost their job. Mark realised that the company had survived one afternoon without him, and that perhaps it could survive more.

From then on, things changed. Mark stopped checking his emails at dinner, and he turned his phone off at weekends. He started leaving work at five on Fridays. And on Saturday mornings, if the weather wasn't too bad, he and Leo went to the park again to feed the ducks and buy hot chocolate from the little van near the gate.
