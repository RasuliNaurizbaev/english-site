The Flat Share

When Maya moved to London for her first job, she couldn't afford to rent a flat on her own. Prices were much higher than she had expected. After three weeks of searching, she found a room in a big old flat above a bakery in the north of the city. Three other people already lived there, and the rent was cheap. The advert had said, "Friendly, quiet flat. Looking for a fourth person." Maya would soon discover that only half of this was true.

The other three people couldn't have been more different. Greg was a forty-year-old taxi driver who had lived in the flat for eight years. He worked at night, slept during the day, and was very serious about rules. Sofia was a music student from Brazil who practised the violin for hours every day. And Kevin was a computer programmer who almost never left his room, except to cook strange meals at two o'clock in the morning.

On her first evening, Greg gave Maya a piece of paper with a list on it. It was titled "Flat Rules" and it had twenty-three points. Nobody was allowed to use the washing machine after nine in the evening. Shoes had to be taken off at the door. Food in the fridge had to be labelled with your name. Maya laughed, thinking it was a joke. Greg didn't laugh.

At first, Maya tried hard to follow all the rules. But it was difficult to live with people who had such different lives. Greg needed silence during the day, but that was exactly when Sofia wanted to practise. Kevin's cooking filled the flat with the smell of fish and burnt onions at night. Sofia never washed her dishes, and they were often left in the sink for days. Kevin borrowed things without asking. Maya found herself in the middle of every argument.

The worst argument happened in November. Greg had come home after a long night shift and found that someone had eaten his cheese, which had been clearly labelled. He knocked loudly on every door and called a "flat meeting" in the kitchen. "I'm tired of this," he said. "I've lived here for eight years, and I've never had such terrible flatmates."

Sofia said that his rules were crazy and that the flat felt like a prison. Kevin said nothing at all and stared at his phone. Maya tried to calm everyone down. In the end, Sofia went to her room and slammed the door, and Greg went to bed. Maya sat alone in the kitchen. She remembered how excited she had been when she arrived. Now she wished she had chosen a different flat. If she had known it would be like this, she would have paid more to live alone.

For the next few weeks, the flatmates barely spoke to each other. They put notes on the fridge instead. "Who left the light on?" "Please stop playing music at 8 o'clock." "Whose socks are these?" Maya started to look at adverts for other rooms. She had even arranged to see one on the following Saturday.

Then, on a freezing night in December, everything changed. Maya woke up at three in the morning because she was cold. She went to the kitchen and noticed a strange smell. At first she thought it was Kevin's cooking again, but this smell was different. It was smoke. It was coming from under the floor, from the bakery downstairs.

She ran from door to door, shouting. Kevin opened his door immediately; he had been awake, as usual. Sofia came out in her pyjamas, confused and half asleep. "Where's Greg?" Maya asked. Then she remembered that Greg was working that night.

Kevin called the fire service while Maya and Sofia grabbed their coats. The smoke was getting thicker. They were about to leave when Sofia stopped. "My violin," she said. It had belonged to her grandfather, and it was the only one she had. Before anyone could stop her, she ran back to her room. Maya was terrified. Kevin went after Sofia, and a few seconds later they both came back, coughing, with the violin case. The three of them ran down the stairs and out into the cold street.

The fire engines arrived within five minutes. The firefighters told them later that an electric oven had been left on in the bakery. The fire had been burning slowly for an hour before Maya noticed the smell. "If you hadn't woken up," a firefighter said to her, "this could have been much worse."

They stood on the pavement in the freezing wind for a long time. Sofia was shaking, and Kevin gave her his jacket. At about four o'clock, a taxi stopped in front of the building. It was Greg. Kevin had called him, and he had driven straight home. When he saw them, he jumped out of the car and ran towards them. "Is everyone all right?" he kept asking. Maya had never seen him look so worried.

Greg made them all get into his taxi to keep warm. He turned on the heating and gave them a bag of biscuits he had been keeping for his break. For a while nobody said anything. Then Kevin said quietly, "I ate your cheese. In November. I'm sorry." There was a short silence. Then Greg started laughing, and soon all four of them were laughing so much that they could hardly breathe.

Luckily, the fire had not reached the flat. Only the bakery kitchen had been damaged. But the flat smelled of smoke, so the four of them had to stay somewhere else for a week. Greg's sister, who had a big house outside the city, invited them all to stay with her.

That week was the first time they had really talked to each other. Maya learned that Greg had been strict about rules because his wife had left him eight years ago, and the flat was the only thing he still felt in control of. Kevin admitted that he had been very lonely since moving from Scotland, and that he cooked at night because he couldn't sleep. Sofia explained that she practised so much because her family had sold their car to pay for her studies. She was afraid of letting them down.

When they moved back into the flat, things were different. They sat down together and wrote new rules. This time there were only five. Sofia would practise in the afternoon, when Greg was already awake. Kevin would open the window when he cooked. Everyone would wash their own dishes. Nobody would eat Greg's cheese. And every Sunday evening they would have dinner together.

Maya cancelled her appointment to see the other room. A year later, she still lives in the flat above the bakery. The Sunday dinners have become the best part of her week. Each person takes a turn to cook, and Sofia usually plays the violin afterwards. Even Greg, who used to complain about the noise, asks her for his favourite song. Sometimes Maya thinks about the advert that brought her there. "Friendly, quiet flat." It still isn't quiet. But now, at last, it is friendly.
